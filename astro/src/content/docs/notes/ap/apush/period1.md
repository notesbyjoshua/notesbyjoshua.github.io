## Native American societies before European contact

The Americas were already home to large, organized societies before Europeans arrived. Native peoples spoke many languages, maintained trade networks, and developed political systems ranging from small kinship communities to large empires. There was no single Native American culture.

### Agriculture and regional differences

**Maize cultivation** spread north from Mesoamerica over many centuries. In places where farming supported a dependable food supply, populations could grow and settlements could become more permanent. Many communities grew maize, beans, and squash together. Beans helped replenish soil nitrogen, while squash leaves helped shade the ground.

Geography shaped the choices available to people, but it did not determine every part of their lives. Communities adapted to changing environments and exchanged goods and knowledge with neighbors.

| Region | Examples and ways of life | Connection to the environment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Southwest | Pueblo peoples built settled communities and practiced agriculture | Irrigation and careful water use supported farming in dry conditions |
| Great Basin | Many communities combined gathering with mobile hunting | Sparse rainfall and scattered resources encouraged seasonal movement |
| Great Plains | Communities combined hunting, gathering, and, in some areas, village agriculture | Grasslands supported bison; river valleys supported farming |
| Pacific Northwest | Fishing communities developed substantial permanent settlements | Salmon and other abundant resources supported settlement without dependence on maize |
| Eastern Woodlands | Many peoples combined farming, hunting, and fishing | Forests and rivers provided food, transport, and building materials |
| Mississippi Valley | Earlier Mississippian societies built towns, ceremonial mounds, and trade networks | Productive agriculture supported social and political specialization |

**Cahokia**, near present-day St. Louis, had been a major Mississippian center centuries before Columbus. Its decline before 1492 does not mean that North America had lacked cities or complex political organization. In the Northeast, the **Haudenosaunee Confederacy** linked several nations through a shared political alliance while preserving the identities of its members.

:::warning
The horse-based Plains cultures often shown in popular images developed after Europeans reintroduced horses to the Americas. Do not project that way of life backward onto all Plains peoples in 1491.
:::

### Land, authority, and exchange

Many Native communities understood land through collective access, use rights, and responsibilities to a community. Practices varied, and Native peoples had territorial boundaries and conflicts. European claims that Indigenous land was simply unused ignored farming, controlled burning, hunting grounds, and established settlements.

Trade connected distant communities through goods such as shells, copper, and stone. These networks also carried political relationships and cultural practices. Native societies were changing before European contact; they were not isolated or frozen in time.

## Why Europeans crossed the Atlantic

European exploration grew out of several developments working together:

- **Commerce:** Merchants and rulers wanted more direct access to Asian luxury goods and new sources of wealth.
- **State competition:** Monarchies sponsored voyages to gain territory, revenue, and advantages over rivals.
- **Religion:** Christian expansion and conversion helped justify conquest. Spain's overseas expansion followed the completion of the Reconquista in 1492.
- **Maritime knowledge:** Improvements in ships, navigation, and mapmaking made long voyages more practical.

Portugal developed routes along Africa and into the Indian Ocean. Spain sponsored Columbus's attempt to reach Asia by sailing west. His voyages instead began sustained exchanges across the Atlantic.

:::key{name="European expansion"}
Economic, political, and religious motives reinforced one another. A ruler could support a voyage to spread Christianity, obtain wealth, and weaken a rival at the same time.
:::

Early English attempts at settlement, including **Roanoke**, struggled. England's later success at Jamestown was not inevitable; colonization required supplies, labor, investment, and workable relationships with the people already living there.

## The Columbian Exchange

The **Columbian Exchange** was the movement of plants, animals, people, and diseases between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres after sustained contact began. It changed diets, environments, population patterns, and labor systems.

| Movement | Examples | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Americas to Europe, Africa, and Asia | Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cacao | New food sources supported population growth and changed diets |
| Europe, Africa, and Asia to the Americas | Wheat, sugarcane, cattle, pigs, horses | New farming systems and animals transformed land use and transportation |
| Disease transmission to the Americas | Smallpox and measles | Epidemics devastated communities with little prior exposure to these diseases |
| Forced migration across the Atlantic | Enslaved Africans | Plantation production expanded through coercion and displacement |

Disease caused catastrophic population losses in many Native communities. Warfare, forced labor, displacement, and food shortages compounded the damage. These losses weakened some communities' ability to resist conquest, but Native peoples continued to negotiate, adapt, and resist.

European livestock could damage Native crops and alter local ecosystems. Sugar production tied land in the Americas to European markets and helped drive demand for enslaved labor. The exchange therefore involved much more than the spread of useful foods.

:::warning
The effects were deeply unequal. New crops could support population growth in one region while epidemic disease and conquest caused severe population decline in another.
:::

## Spanish conquest and colonial society

### Conquest and Native alliances

**Hernán Cortés** defeated the Mexica Empire with crucial support from Indigenous enemies of Mexica rule. Disease and political divisions also contributed. **Francisco Pizarro** later conquered the Inca Empire during a period of internal conflict.

European weapons mattered, but a simple story of a few Europeans defeating entire empires leaves out Native allies, epidemics, and local political struggles. Indigenous participants pursued their own goals; they were not merely supporting characters in European expansion.

### Labor and wealth

Spain extracted wealth through agriculture and mining, especially silver. Colonial labor systems placed heavy demands on Indigenous communities.

- **Encomienda:** The crown granted colonists claims to Indigenous tribute and labor, supposedly in exchange for protection and Christian instruction. In practice, it enabled severe exploitation.
- **Repartimiento:** Officials required communities to supply workers for periods of labor. Payment did not make the system voluntary.
- **African slavery:** Colonists increasingly exploited enslaved Africans, especially where plantation agriculture demanded large labor forces.

:::key{name="Labor systems"}
An encomienda was a grant of claims to Indigenous labor and tribute, not simply a grant of land. Keep the labor arrangement separate from the question of who held land.
:::

### Social hierarchy and cultural change

Spanish colonial society ranked people partly by ancestry, birthplace, legal status, and wealth. People born in Spain generally held advantages over American-born Spaniards. Categories such as **mestizo** and **mulatto** reflected mixed ancestry, but actual social position was more complicated than a fixed chart of racial categories.

Catholic missions promoted conversion and attempted to reshape Native life. Indigenous peoples sometimes adopted Christian practices while maintaining or adapting older traditions. Africans also preserved and developed cultural practices under coercive conditions. Colonial culture emerged through unequal exchanges, resistance, and adaptation.

## Debates over conquest and Native resistance

Spanish expansion produced arguments over whether conquest and forced labor could be justified. **Bartolomé de las Casas** criticized colonists' treatment of Native peoples. **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** defended conquest using claims of Indigenous inferiority. Their dispute formed part of the **Valladolid debate** in 1550–1551.

Spanish reform laws attempted to limit some abuses, but colonial resistance and weak enforcement restricted their effects. Criticism within the empire did not end exploitation.

Native responses included military resistance, diplomatic agreements, strategic alliances, migration, and selective adoption of European goods. A community might trade with Europeans while resisting missionaries or territorial expansion. Cooperation in one area did not mean acceptance of colonial rule in every area.

:::tip
When explaining an encounter, identify the goals of each group. “Europeans arrived and Native peoples reacted” is too broad to explain why some communities fought while others formed alliances.
:::

## Connections and recap

| Historical connection | Evidence from this period |
| --- | --- |
| Environment and society | Irrigation in the Southwest; fishing settlements in the Pacific Northwest |
| Causes of conquest | European state competition, Indigenous alliances, disease, and military force |
| Economic change | Atlantic exchange, silver mining, and plantation labor |
| Continuity and change | Native societies adapted while maintaining distinct identities and political goals |

:::summary{title="Period 1"}
Before contact, the Americas contained diverse societies connected by trade and shaped by regional environments. Atlantic contact brought new goods and opportunities alongside epidemics, conquest, and coerced labor. By 1607, Spain had built a substantial empire, while England was beginning a permanent foothold on the Atlantic coast.
:::
