## European colonies and Native relationships

European empires pursued different colonial strategies, depending on their resources, goals, and the Native societies they encountered.

| Empire | Main activities | Common patterns of interaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spain | Missions, mining, ranching, and settlements | Conversion and coerced labor; Native resistance and cultural adaptation |
| France | Fur trade and settlements along waterways | Commercial and military alliances with Native nations; missionary activity |
| Netherlands | Atlantic commerce and the fur trade | Trading relationships alongside conflict over land |
| England | Agricultural settlement and Atlantic trade | Growing settler populations placed sustained pressure on Native land |

These are broad patterns, not absolute rules. French and Dutch colonization also involved violence, and English colonists also depended on diplomacy and trade. Native nations used European rivalries to pursue their own interests.

In New Mexico, **Popé** helped organize the **Pueblo Revolt of 1680** against Spanish religious repression and colonial demands. The revolt drove the Spanish out for more than a decade. Spanish authorities later returned, but the rebellion forced adjustments in colonial rule.

:::warning
Native peoples did not act as a single group. Alliances followed local interests, rivalries, and changing threats rather than a simple division between “Europeans” and “Native Americans.”
:::

## The Chesapeake and the southern colonies

### Tobacco, land, and labor

Jamestown initially faced disease, hunger, leadership problems, and conflict with the **Powhatan Confederacy**. Tobacco became a profitable export, but its production required land and labor. English expansion consequently intensified pressure on nearby Native communities.

The **headright system** rewarded those who financed migration with land grants. Wealthy colonists could accumulate large holdings by paying for many workers' passages. **Indentured servants** agreed to work for a set term in exchange for transportation and support. Their contracts ended, but harsh conditions meant that freedom and prosperity were far from guaranteed.

Virginia's **House of Burgesses**, first assembled in 1619, gave some colonists a voice in government. Participation remained limited, especially by sex, property, and legal status. Representative institutions could coexist with deep inequality.

### From servitude to racial slavery

The legal position of Africans in the early Chesapeake varied. Over the seventeenth century, colonial laws increasingly defined African-descended people as enslaved for life and made slavery hereditary. Virginia's 1662 law tied a child's status to the mother's status, allowing enslavers to claim children born to enslaved women.

Several factors encouraged the growth of racial slavery: plantation demand, the availability of enslaved Africans through Atlantic trade, changing supplies of indentured servants, and elites' efforts to secure a permanent labor force.

**Bacon's Rebellion** in 1676 combined resentment of Virginia's governor with demands for more aggressive attacks on Native peoples. The rebellion exposed tensions between frontier settlers and established elites. It contributed to elite fears of instability, but slavery was already developing before the uprising.

:::key{name="Bacon's Rebellion"}
Do not treat 1676 as the single moment when indentured servitude became slavery. The shift occurred over decades through economic choices, racial laws, and political conflict.
:::

Farther south, rice and indigo supported plantation economies in the Carolinas. Enslaved Africans contributed agricultural knowledge as well as labor. Plantation districts often had large African-descended populations, enabling the development of distinctive communities and traditions despite brutal coercion. Georgia began as a buffer against Spanish Florida and initially restricted slavery, but later joined the plantation system.

## New England and the middle colonies

### Religion and community in New England

**Separatists** at Plymouth wanted to separate from the Church of England. **Puritans** in Massachusetts Bay sought to build a reformed Christian society. Migration by families, relatively longer life expectancies, and town-based settlement helped create stable communities.

New England's economy combined small farms, fishing, shipbuilding, and commerce. Town meetings provided local participation for eligible men, while religious and political leaders enforced social conformity.

- **Roger Williams** criticized religious coercion and challenged colonial claims to Native land. He established Rhode Island after banishment from Massachusetts.
- **Anne Hutchinson** challenged religious authorities and was also banished.
- The **Half-Way Covenant** relaxed some church membership requirements as ministers worried about declining participation.

:::warning
Seeking religious freedom for one's own community did not necessarily mean supporting religious freedom for everyone. Puritan Massachusetts punished dissent, while Rhode Island allowed broader religious liberty.
:::

### Diversity in the middle colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware had comparatively diverse populations and economies. Grain farming supplied Atlantic markets, while ports such as Philadelphia and New York supported trade and skilled work.

**William Penn's Pennsylvania**, shaped by Quaker beliefs, offered broad religious toleration for its time. Early relations with Native peoples were often more cooperative than in neighboring colonies, although later population growth and land disputes produced conflict.

| Region | Economic base | Settlement and society |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New England | Small farms, fishing, shipping, and commerce | Towns, family migration, strong Congregational influence |
| Middle colonies | Grain, commerce, and crafts | Religious and ethnic diversity; important port cities |
| Chesapeake | Tobacco | Dispersed plantations, land inequality, growing slavery |
| Lower South | Rice and indigo | Plantation agriculture with a large enslaved population |

## Atlantic trade and slavery

The colonies participated in an **Atlantic economy** connecting Europe, Africa, the Caribbean, and mainland North America. Manufactured goods, agricultural products, enslaved people, and capital moved through overlapping routes.

The **Middle Passage** was the forced Atlantic voyage of enslaved Africans. Captives endured confinement, disease, violence, and high mortality. Enslaved people resisted through rebellion, escape, work slowdowns, and the preservation of family and cultural ties.

The **Stono Rebellion** in South Carolina in 1739 was a major uprising against slavery. Colonial authorities responded with tighter restrictions, showing how resistance and repression shaped slave societies together.

:::note
“Triangular trade” is a useful diagram for some exchanges, but no single triangle describes the whole Atlantic economy. Many ships traveled between only two ports, and Caribbean trade was especially important to mainland merchants.
:::

Northern colonies also benefited from slavery. Enslaved labor existed there, and merchants supplied plantations, transported goods, and participated in the slave trade. Regional differences in the scale of slavery did not make northern economies independent of it.

## Britain and colonial government

**Mercantilism** treated colonies as resources for increasing the wealth and power of the empire. The **Navigation Acts** regulated shipping and trade to benefit England. Colonial merchants sometimes complied and sometimes smuggled goods to avoid restrictions.

Enforcement varied. The pattern often called **salutary neglect** allowed colonial assemblies and local elites considerable practical autonomy during parts of the colonial period. It did not mean Britain had no laws or that the colonies were independent.

The **Dominion of New England** attempted to centralize control in the 1680s. Colonists resented restrictions on local institutions and the rule of Edmund Andros. News of England's **Glorious Revolution** helped trigger the Dominion's overthrow in 1689.

Colonial assemblies became accustomed to controlling local taxation and spending. That experience later shaped resistance when Britain tried to tighten imperial control after 1763.

## Cultural change and conflict

### Enlightenment and the First Great Awakening

The **Enlightenment** emphasized reason, observation, and the possibility of understanding society through natural laws. Thinkers such as John Locke influenced arguments about natural rights and government by consent.

The **First Great Awakening** emphasized conversion and an emotionally powerful personal relationship with God. Preachers such as Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield attracted large audiences. Disputes between supporters and critics divided congregations and helped expand religious choice.

:::key{name="Challenging authority"}
The Enlightenment and Great Awakening differed in method: one emphasized reason, the other religious experience. Both could encourage people to question inherited authority, but neither automatically produced support for independence.
:::

British goods, newspapers, and social customs spread through the colonies in a process often called **Anglicization**. Colonists could develop local institutions while still identifying strongly as British.

### Conflict on the frontier

The **Pequot War** and **Metacom's War**, also called King Philip's War, grew from struggles over land, power, and survival. Metacom's War caused enormous losses and weakened Native independence in much of southern New England. Native resistance continued elsewhere.

As colonists pushed inland, competition among Britain, France, and Native nations intensified. The Ohio Valley became a flashpoint because it connected trade routes and competing territorial claims.

## Connections and recap

| Historical connection | Evidence from this period |
| --- | --- |
| Regional differences | Tobacco plantations, New England towns, and middle-colony grain farms |
| Freedom and exclusion | Elected assemblies alongside slavery and religious restrictions |
| Imperial integration | Navigation Acts, Atlantic trade, and Anglicization |
| Sources of later conflict | Local political autonomy and competition over western land |

:::summary{title="Period 2"}
British colonies developed distinct regional economies but remained connected through trade and imperial rule. Representative government expanded for some colonists while racial slavery became more entrenched. Native nations continued to shape diplomacy and warfare as European settlement spread inland.
:::
