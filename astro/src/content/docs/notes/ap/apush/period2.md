## European colonies and Native relationships

European empires pursued different colonial strategies, depending on their resources, goals, and the Native societies they encountered.

| Empire | Main activities | Common patterns of interaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spain | Missions, mining, ranching, and settlements | Conversion and coerced labor; Native resistance and cultural adaptation |
| France | Fur trade and settlements along waterways | Commercial and military alliances with Native nations; missionary activity |
| Netherlands | Atlantic commerce and the fur trade | Trading relationships alongside conflict over land |
| England | Agricultural settlement and Atlantic trade | Growing settler populations placed sustained pressure on Native land |

These are broad patterns, not absolute rules. French and Dutch colonization also involved violence, and English colonists also depended on diplomacy and trade. Native nations used European rivalries to pursue their own interests.

In New Mexico, **Popé** helped organize the **Pueblo Revolt of 1680** against Spanish religious repression and colonial demands. The revolt drove the Spanish out for more than a decade. Spanish authorities later returned, but the rebellion forced adjustments in colonial rule.

:::warning
Native peoples did not act as a single group. Alliances followed local interests, rivalries, and changing threats rather than a simple division between “Europeans” and “Native Americans.”
:::

### Trade, settlement, and the balance of power

The type of colonial economy affected relations with Native peoples. A fur trader needed access to hunters, transportation networks, and diplomatic partners. A farming family seeking permanent possession of land could regard those same partners as obstacles. This distinction helps explain broad differences between French commercial alliances and the land conflicts accompanying large English settlements.

Trade could increase Native bargaining power when several European buyers competed. Native leaders sometimes played one empire against another to obtain better prices, weapons, or military support. But reliance on imported goods could also create vulnerability, especially if warfare disrupted access or colonial authorities tried to impose unfavorable terms.

European goods changed relationships within Native communities as well. Access to firearms could strengthen one group relative to its neighbors. Demand for furs encouraged hunting for distant markets and sometimes depleted animal populations. These changes could intensify conflict without making Native people passive victims of commerce; communities continued to make strategic choices within increasingly constrained circumstances.

French settlers and Native peoples in the Great Lakes region sometimes developed negotiated customs for exchange and diplomacy. Historians describe such arrangements as a **middle ground**, where neither side initially possessed complete control and each adapted to the expectations of the other. Cooperation depended on maintaining that balance. Growing settler populations or a decisive imperial victory could undermine the need to negotiate.

### Comparing colonial priorities

Spanish missions sought conversion and more direct supervision of Native labor and settlement. French fur-trading communities often remained smaller and more dependent on alliances. Dutch New Netherland centered on commerce but also promoted landholding and settlement, which created its own conflicts. English colonies varied widely, yet their rapidly growing settler population made territorial expansion a persistent source of tension.

These comparisons work best when they connect an economic activity to a political relationship. Saying that the French were simply “friendly” and the English “hostile” turns changing strategic relationships into national personality traits. French alliances involved war against other Native nations, and English colonies sometimes maintained long periods of cooperation with particular neighbors.

:::key{name="Native diplomacy"}
An alliance with a European empire was usually a means to pursue Native goals, including security, trade, or advantage over rivals. It was not necessarily an endorsement of that empire's claim to govern Native land.
:::

## The Chesapeake and the southern colonies

### Tobacco, land, and labor

Jamestown initially faced disease, hunger, leadership problems, and conflict with the **Powhatan Confederacy**. Tobacco became a profitable export, but its production required land and labor. English expansion consequently intensified pressure on nearby Native communities.

The **headright system** rewarded those who financed migration with land grants. Wealthy colonists could accumulate large holdings by paying for many workers' passages. **Indentured servants** agreed to work for a set term in exchange for transportation and support. Their contracts ended, but harsh conditions meant that freedom and prosperity were far from guaranteed.

Virginia's **House of Burgesses**, first assembled in 1619, gave some colonists a voice in government. Participation remained limited, especially by sex, property, and legal status. Representative institutions could coexist with deep inequality.

### Why early Virginia was unstable

The **Virginia Company**, a joint-stock venture, expected profits from settlement. Its commercial ambitions initially fit poorly with the immediate need to grow food and maintain reliable supplies. Disease, conflict, and shortages made survival uncertain. Colonists depended on exchanges with Powhatan communities even as they tried to secure independence from those relationships.

The Powhatan Confederacy was a political network with its own leaders, tribute relationships, and territorial priorities. Powhatan initially had reasons to incorporate or manage the newcomers rather than immediately destroy them. English demands for food and expanding tobacco cultivation made cooperation increasingly difficult. The Anglo-Powhatan wars reflected this struggle over who would control resources and settlement.

Tobacco changed the colony's incentives. More production could bring more revenue, but it also required fresh land and workers. Because prices fluctuated, planters might respond to falling prices by planting more, intensifying competition and expansion. The crop connected local land disputes to consumer demand across the Atlantic.

### Household formation and inequality

Early Chesapeake migration included many young male servants. Disease and uneven sex ratios made family formation less stable than in New England. As population patterns changed, established families and large landowners consolidated power. Wealth allowed planters to buy labor, acquire more acreage, and dominate county institutions.

Servitude and slavery both involved coercion, but their legal structures differed. An indentured servant had a contract with a defined endpoint, although employers could obtain extensions for alleged misconduct. An enslaved person's labor and descendants could be claimed permanently. The distinction shaped whether a worker could eventually establish an independent household and pass property to children.

Neither experience should be reduced to a promise of opportunity. Former servants could emerge free but without enough land or capital to compete with established planters. Their movement toward frontier land could then intensify conflict with Native communities and colonial officials.

### From servitude to racial slavery

The legal position of Africans in the early Chesapeake varied. Over the seventeenth century, colonial laws increasingly defined African-descended people as enslaved for life and made slavery hereditary. Virginia's 1662 law tied a child's status to the mother's status, allowing enslavers to claim children born to enslaved women.

Several factors encouraged the growth of racial slavery: plantation demand, the availability of enslaved Africans through Atlantic trade, changing supplies of indentured servants, and elites' efforts to secure a permanent labor force.

**Bacon's Rebellion** in 1676 combined resentment of Virginia's governor with demands for more aggressive attacks on Native peoples. The rebellion exposed tensions between frontier settlers and established elites. It contributed to elite fears of instability, but slavery was already developing before the uprising.

:::key{name="Bacon's Rebellion"}
Do not treat 1676 as the single moment when indentured servitude became slavery. The shift occurred over decades through economic choices, racial laws, and political conflict.
:::

Farther south, rice and indigo supported plantation economies in the Carolinas. Enslaved Africans contributed agricultural knowledge as well as labor. Plantation districts often had large African-descended populations, enabling the development of distinctive communities and traditions despite brutal coercion. Georgia began as a buffer against Spanish Florida and initially restricted slavery, but later joined the plantation system.

### Law and the construction of racial status

Colonial legislators gradually made racial categories central to labor control. Laws distinguished the treatment of European servants from that of enslaved Africans, restricted interracial relationships, and limited opportunities for freedom. Making a child's status follow the mother ensured that enslavers could claim children even when the father was free or white.

The development of slavery was therefore political as well as economic. Profit alone did not specify which people could be held for life, whether conversion changed legal status, or whether children inherited enslavement. Laws answered those questions in ways that protected enslavers' interests and made racial inequality appear permanent.

Slaveholding elites also offered legal advantages to white laborers that did not extend to Black people. These distinctions could reduce the appeal of cooperation across racial lines. They did not eliminate class conflict among whites, but they strengthened a social order in which even a poor white person possessed rights denied to an enslaved person.

### Caribbean connections to the Lower South

The mainland colonies did not develop separately from Caribbean plantation societies. Settlers moving from places such as Barbados brought capital, experience with plantation management, and harsh slave codes. South Carolina's development reflected these connections, while rice cultivation also depended on the knowledge and labor of enslaved Africans.

Caribbean sugar islands created demand for mainland food, lumber, livestock, and ships. A New England merchant or middle-colony farmer could profit from plantation slavery without personally growing sugar. This connection explains why slavery's economic reach extended far beyond the areas where enslaved people formed the largest share of the population.

## New England and the middle colonies

### Religion and community in New England

**Separatists** at Plymouth wanted to separate from the Church of England. **Puritans** in Massachusetts Bay sought to build a reformed Christian society. Migration by families, relatively longer life expectancies, and town-based settlement helped create stable communities.

New England's economy combined small farms, fishing, shipbuilding, and commerce. Town meetings provided local participation for eligible men, while religious and political leaders enforced social conformity.

- **Roger Williams** criticized religious coercion and challenged colonial claims to Native land. He established Rhode Island after banishment from Massachusetts.
- **Anne Hutchinson** challenged religious authorities and was also banished.
- The **Half-Way Covenant** relaxed some church membership requirements as ministers worried about declining participation.

:::warning
Seeking religious freedom for one's own community did not necessarily mean supporting religious freedom for everyone. Puritan Massachusetts punished dissent, while Rhode Island allowed broader religious liberty.
:::

### Covenants, education, and political authority

The **Mayflower Compact** was an agreement among Plymouth's male signers to establish a political community and obey its laws. It is evidence of government organized through agreement, but it was not a modern constitution guaranteeing equal participation to everyone. Its signers still understood themselves as subjects of the English monarch.

Puritan communities linked social order to a covenant with God. Leaders believed that misconduct could endanger the whole community, which helps explain their willingness to regulate behavior and punish dissent. The ideal of a model Christian society made religious disagreement a political concern rather than merely a private difference of belief.

Literacy mattered because Protestants emphasized reading Scripture. Schools and colleges helped train ministers and sustain religious communities. Over time, education also supplied skills useful for commerce and public life. An institution founded for religious purposes could therefore have consequences extending beyond its original goal.

The **Salem witch trials** of 1692 reveal the dangers of fear, accusation, and unstable authority. Religious beliefs helped make accusations credible, while local tensions shaped who became vulnerable. The trials should not stand in for all New England life, but they show how conflict could escalate when communities accepted weak evidence during a crisis.

:::warning
Town meetings and representative assemblies were important precedents for later self-government. They did not make colonial society democratic in the modern sense: participation depended on gender, status, property, and sometimes religious membership.
:::

### Diversity in the middle colonies

New York, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, and Delaware had comparatively diverse populations and economies. Grain farming supplied Atlantic markets, while ports such as Philadelphia and New York supported trade and skilled work.

**William Penn's Pennsylvania**, shaped by Quaker beliefs, offered broad religious toleration for its time. Early relations with Native peoples were often more cooperative than in neighboring colonies, although later population growth and land disputes produced conflict.

| Region | Economic base | Settlement and society |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New England | Small farms, fishing, shipping, and commerce | Towns, family migration, strong Congregational influence |
| Middle colonies | Grain, commerce, and crafts | Religious and ethnic diversity; important port cities |
| Chesapeake | Tobacco | Dispersed plantations, land inequality, growing slavery |
| Lower South | Rice and indigo | Plantation agriculture with a large enslaved population |

### Toleration had different meanings

**Maryland** began as a proprietary colony associated with the Catholic Calvert family. Its Act of Toleration protected certain Christians, not every religious belief, and the colony experienced repeated Protestant-Catholic conflict. This makes it a useful contrast with both Puritan Massachusetts and Rhode Island's broader protection of conscience.

Pennsylvania's Quaker influence encouraged religious toleration and relatively cooperative early diplomacy. Quakers rejected some conventional religious hierarchies and placed importance on conscience. Yet the colony still existed within an imperial system, and later settlers' appetite for land weakened earlier commitments to fair dealing with Native peoples.

The middle colonies' diversity made a single established religious culture harder to impose. Commercial cities brought together people who needed to work and trade despite religious differences. Economic cooperation did not erase prejudice, but it could create practical reasons to accept a degree of pluralism.

Regional comparison should also include variation within regions. A wealthy Philadelphia merchant and a frontier farmer did not have identical interests. Nor did a New England port resemble every inland town. The regional categories are useful patterns, not descriptions of every individual colonist.

## Atlantic trade and slavery

The colonies participated in an **Atlantic economy** connecting Europe, Africa, the Caribbean, and mainland North America. Manufactured goods, agricultural products, enslaved people, and capital moved through overlapping routes.

The **Middle Passage** was the forced Atlantic voyage of enslaved Africans. Captives endured confinement, disease, violence, and high mortality. Enslaved people resisted through rebellion, escape, work slowdowns, and the preservation of family and cultural ties.

The **Stono Rebellion** in South Carolina in 1739 was a major uprising against slavery. Colonial authorities responded with tighter restrictions, showing how resistance and repression shaped slave societies together.

:::note
“Triangular trade” is a useful diagram for some exchanges, but no single triangle describes the whole Atlantic economy. Many ships traveled between only two ports, and Caribbean trade was especially important to mainland merchants.
:::

Northern colonies also benefited from slavery. Enslaved labor existed there, and merchants supplied plantations, transported goods, and participated in the slave trade. Regional differences in the scale of slavery did not make northern economies independent of it.

### Enslaved communities and resistance

Enslaved people worked in fields, homes, workshops, ports, and skilled trades. Conditions depended on the crop, size of the enslaved population, and labor system. In some rice-growing areas, the **task system** assigned a specified amount of daily work; completion could leave limited time for personal cultivation or exchange. Tobacco plantations often used closely supervised group labor. Neither arrangement removed the threat of violence or sale.

Family and community life required constant effort because enslavers could separate people through sale or inheritance. Enslaved people created kinship ties that extended beyond biological households and drew on African as well as American experiences. Religion, music, naming practices, and oral traditions helped sustain identity under conditions designed to deny autonomy.

Resistance did not always take the form of a large uprising. People could feign illness, damage tools, withhold knowledge, escape temporarily, or negotiate over tasks. These actions challenged control even when they did not end enslavement. Large revolts were especially difficult because armed authorities, surveillance, and the danger to families limited options.

Stono's participants moved toward Spanish Florida, where Spanish policy offered freedom under certain conditions to people escaping British slavery. Their choices show how imperial rivalry could create openings within a slave society. Colonial authorities responded by restricting movement, assembly, and education, demonstrating that slave codes often tightened in response to enslaved people's actions.

:::key{name="Agency under coercion"}
Recognizing resistance does not mean enslaved people had free choices. It means they pursued goals and shaped events within a system that used law and violence to limit their options.
:::

## Britain and colonial government

**Mercantilism** treated colonies as resources for increasing the wealth and power of the empire. The **Navigation Acts** regulated shipping and trade to benefit England. Colonial merchants sometimes complied and sometimes smuggled goods to avoid restrictions.

Enforcement varied. The pattern often called **salutary neglect** allowed colonial assemblies and local elites considerable practical autonomy during parts of the colonial period. It did not mean Britain had no laws or that the colonies were independent.

The **Dominion of New England** attempted to centralize control in the 1680s. Colonists resented restrictions on local institutions and the rule of Edmund Andros. News of England's **Glorious Revolution** helped trigger the Dominion's overthrow in 1689.

Colonial assemblies became accustomed to controlling local taxation and spending. That experience later shaped resistance when Britain tried to tighten imperial control after 1763.

### Who actually governed a colony?

Colonial government usually involved overlapping authorities: a governor, a council, an elected assembly, local officials, and the imperial government. In royal colonies, governors represented the crown, but assemblies could use their control of money to influence policy and salaries. Formal authority and practical bargaining power were not always located in the same place.

Proprietary colonies belonged to individuals or families under royal grants, while charter arrangements could permit greater local autonomy. These differences affected institutions, but colonists throughout British America developed experience in managing local affairs. When later imperial reforms threatened that experience, colonists could argue that they were defending established rights rather than inventing an entirely new political order.

The **English Bill of Rights** of 1689 limited royal power and reinforced the importance of Parliament. Colonists drew on this broader English tradition when discussing taxation, representation, and arbitrary authority. Their political expectations were initially British expectations, not necessarily expressions of an already separate American nationality.

### Mercantilism in practice

Trade restrictions aimed to direct colonial wealth through English shipping and markets. Specified commodities had to move through approved channels, and imperial rules could constrain colonial manufacturing or trade with rival empires. Yet colonists also benefited from access to British markets, naval protection, and commercial credit.

The relationship therefore combined benefits and restrictions. A merchant could identify as loyal to Britain while smuggling goods or opposing a particular customs rule. Conflict became more serious when authorities changed enforcement or claimed powers that colonists believed violated accepted practice.

“Salutary neglect” should be used carefully because enforcement was inconsistent across places and decades. The Dominion of New England was an obvious effort to impose tighter control before the eighteenth century. The larger pattern was an empire whose laws claimed extensive authority but whose ability and willingness to enforce them varied.

:::note
An empire can become more culturally connected while remaining politically decentralized. British fashions and consumer goods spread even as colonial assemblies gained experience governing local affairs.
:::

## Cultural change and conflict

### Enlightenment and the First Great Awakening

The **Enlightenment** emphasized reason, observation, and the possibility of understanding society through natural laws. Thinkers such as John Locke influenced arguments about natural rights and government by consent.

The **First Great Awakening** emphasized conversion and an emotionally powerful personal relationship with God. Preachers such as Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield attracted large audiences. Disputes between supporters and critics divided congregations and helped expand religious choice.

:::key{name="Challenging authority"}
The Enlightenment and Great Awakening differed in method: one emphasized reason, the other religious experience. Both could encourage people to question inherited authority, but neither automatically produced support for independence.
:::

British goods, newspapers, and social customs spread through the colonies in a process often called **Anglicization**. Colonists could develop local institutions while still identifying strongly as British.

### Revival, print, and a wider public

Revival preaching crossed colony boundaries, bringing people into shared religious debates. Traveling ministers could attract listeners who were dissatisfied with established congregations. **New Lights** welcomed revivals, while **Old Lights** criticized emotional preaching and challenges to trained clergy. The disagreement fractured some churches while encouraging new institutions.

The Awakening could elevate personal experience over established status: a listener's conversion mattered more than wealth or an inherited position. That did not automatically produce social equality, but it gave some people grounds to challenge ministers and older hierarchies. Evangelical religion could support criticism of oppression while also coexisting with slaveholding.

Print culture made wider discussion possible. Newspapers, pamphlets, sermons, and almanacs circulated arguments beyond face-to-face communities. **Benjamin Franklin** exemplified the connections among practical learning, commerce, publishing, and Enlightenment curiosity. Expanding literacy and print did not give everyone equal influence, but they increased the reach of political and religious debate.

These developments help explain how separate colonies could later coordinate protest. They already had networks through which ideas, people, and information traveled. Cooperation during the Revolution did not arise from a continent of completely isolated settlements.

### Conflict on the frontier

The **Pequot War** and **Metacom's War**, also called King Philip's War, grew from struggles over land, power, and survival. Metacom's War caused enormous losses and weakened Native independence in much of southern New England. Native resistance continued elsewhere.

As colonists pushed inland, competition among Britain, France, and Native nations intensified. The Ohio Valley became a flashpoint because it connected trade routes and competing territorial claims.

### Frontier warfare and imperial competition

The **Pequot War** involved English colonists and Native allies fighting the Pequots. The attack at Mystic and the war's aftermath devastated Pequot communities. The participation of Native allies matters: warfare followed local rivalries and strategic choices, not a simple racial division in which all Native nations acted together.

Metacom's War later involved a wider struggle over colonial expansion in New England. Heavy losses, enslavement, and displacement damaged Native political independence, while colonists also suffered destruction of settlements and loss of life. Victory increased colonial power but did not erase Native communities or their claims to land.

By the eighteenth century, imperial wars repeatedly involved North America in conflicts originating partly in Europe. Forts, trade routes, and alliances became tools of competition. In the Ohio Valley, British settlers' land ambitions collided with French claims and Native efforts to preserve autonomy. A trading partner who wanted goods could become a territorial threat when settlers followed.

The **Albany Plan of Union** in 1754 proposed colonial cooperation under a common structure but failed to gain acceptance. Its failure shows how strong separate colonial interests remained on the eve of the French and Indian War. Shared British identity and common concerns did not yet produce willingness to surrender much local authority.
