## The French and Indian War and the imperial crisis

The **French and Indian War** began as a struggle over the Ohio Valley and became part of the global Seven Years' War. Britain and France relied on Native alliances; Native nations chose partners according to their own territorial and political interests.

Britain's victory in 1763 removed France as a major mainland rival in eastern North America. Britain gained Canada and French claims east of the Mississippi, except New Orleans, while Spain received Louisiana west of the river and New Orleans. Spain ceded Florida to Britain.

Victory also left Britain with debt and the cost of managing an enlarged empire. British leaders believed colonists should help pay for their own defense. Many colonists believed their wartime service entitled them to greater autonomy and access to western land.

**Pontiac's War** demonstrated Native resistance to British expansion. The **Proclamation of 1763** attempted to limit colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains. Colonists who wanted land saw the restriction as an obstacle, while Britain viewed it as a way to reduce conflict and expense.

:::key{name="The 1763 turning point"}
The war strengthened Britain's empire but weakened its relationship with the colonies. New revenue demands and frontier controls disrupted expectations built during decades of local autonomy.
:::

## From protest to independence

### Taxes and representation

Colonists argued that Parliament could not tax them without their consent through elected representatives. Parliament defended **virtual representation**, claiming its members represented the interests of all British subjects, including those who could not vote for them.

| British measure | Purpose or effect | Colonial response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sugar Act, 1764 | Raises revenue and strengthens customs enforcement | Merchants resist tighter regulation |
| Stamp Act, 1765 | Taxes printed materials | Stamp Act Congress, boycotts, and intimidation of stamp distributors |
| Declaratory Act, 1766 | Asserts Parliament's authority after Stamp Act repeal | Leaves the constitutional dispute unresolved |
| Townshend duties, 1767 | Taxes selected imports | Renewed nonimportation and protests |
| Tea Act, 1773 | Helps the East India Company sell tea while retaining the tea tax | Boston Tea Party challenges the arrangement |
| Coercive Acts, 1774 | Punishes Massachusetts after the Tea Party | Colonies coordinate through the First Continental Congress |

The **Sons of Liberty**, colonial newspapers, and **committees of correspondence** helped organize opposition. Women participated through boycotts and the production of homespun cloth. Resistance depended on everyday economic choices as well as elite political arguments.

The **Boston Massacre** in 1770 became a powerful propaganda symbol. The **Boston Tea Party** in 1773 and Britain's response made compromise harder.

### The decision to separate

Fighting began at **Lexington and Concord** in April 1775, but independence was not yet a universal goal. The **Olive Branch Petition** still sought reconciliation. Thomas Paine's **Common Sense** in 1776 attacked monarchy and made separation seem both reasonable and necessary.

The **Declaration of Independence** justified separation through natural rights, consent of the governed, and a list of grievances against the king. It drew on Enlightenment ideas and earlier traditions of resistance to arbitrary power.

:::warning
Do not assume everyone who opposed a tax supported independence. Positions changed as violence escalated, reconciliation failed, and the costs of remaining in the empire became clearer.
:::

## The Revolutionary War

Britain possessed a powerful navy, an experienced army, and substantial resources. Patriots benefited from local knowledge, the ability to fight a defensive war, and the difficulty Britain faced in controlling a large territory across the Atlantic.

**George Washington** kept the Continental Army together despite shortages and defeats. Victory did not require winning every battle; the army had to survive long enough to make British control too costly.

The American victory at **Saratoga** in 1777 helped secure a formal alliance with France. French money, troops, and naval power were decisive. At **Yorktown** in 1781, American and French forces trapped Cornwallis while the French fleet blocked escape by sea. The **Treaty of Paris of 1783** recognized independence and U.S. territory extending to the Mississippi River.

### A divided population

- **Patriots** supported independence, though they differed over the future republic.
- **Loyalists** remained attached to Britain for political, economic, religious, or personal reasons.
- **Enslaved people** pursued freedom through escape and service to either side when opportunities arose. British offers of freedom were limited and tied to military needs.
- **Native nations** often viewed a British victory as the better chance to limit settler expansion, but communities made different choices and sometimes split internally.
- **Women** maintained farms and businesses, supplied armies, and sometimes participated directly in military activity.

:::note
The peace settlement transferred imperial claims without securing Native consent. Independence expanded U.S. claims to western land while threatening Native sovereignty.
:::

## Revolutionary ideals and their limits

**Republicanism** held that legitimate government depended on the people and required civic virtue rather than hereditary monarchy. State constitutions expanded participation in some places, but property requirements and other exclusions remained.

Revolutionary language encouraged challenges to slavery. Several northern states adopted gradual emancipation, while slavery remained deeply rooted in the South. Free Black communities used the language of liberty to demand rights, but emancipation did not produce racial equality.

**Republican motherhood** argued that women needed education to raise virtuous citizens. This could support female education while keeping women's recognized political role within the household. It did not establish equal voting or legal rights.

Religious disestablishment also advanced. Virginia's **Statute for Religious Freedom** rejected state-compelled religious support, helping establish a model for religious liberty.

:::key{name="Change and continuity"}
The Revolution changed the source of political authority and opened new arguments about rights. It did not eliminate slavery, Native dispossession, or the exclusion of women from formal political power.
:::

## The Articles of Confederation

The **Articles of Confederation** created a loose union with a weak central government. Americans feared replacing British tyranny with another powerful national authority.

Congress could conduct diplomacy and war, but it could not directly tax individuals or effectively regulate interstate commerce. Major decisions required broad state agreement, and amendments required unanimity. There was no separate national executive or national judicial branch comparable to those later created by the Constitution.

The Confederation nevertheless established important western policies:

- **Land Ordinance of 1785:** Created a survey system for organizing and selling western land.
- **Northwest Ordinance of 1787:** Established a process for territorial government and statehood and prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory.

These measures encouraged expansion, but they treated Native lands as territory available for U.S. settlement.

**Shays' Rebellion** in 1786–1787 grew from debt, taxation, and foreclosure pressures in Massachusetts. Its suppression did not prove the country had collapsed, but the uprising strengthened arguments that the central government needed more capacity to maintain stability.

## The Constitution and ratification

The **Constitutional Convention** of 1787 replaced the Articles with a system combining national authority, state governments, and limits on power.

| Principle or compromise | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Federalism | Power is divided between national and state governments |
| Separation of powers | Legislative, executive, and judicial functions belong to different branches |
| Checks and balances | Each branch can restrain actions of the others |
| Great Compromise | Population-based representation in the House; equal state representation in the Senate |
| Three-Fifths Compromise | Three-fifths of the enslaved population counts for representation and direct taxation |
| Slave-trade compromise | Congress cannot prohibit the international slave trade before 1808 |

The Three-Fifths Compromise increased slaveholding states' political power without giving enslaved people political rights. The Constitution also required the return of people escaping from slavery.

**Federalists** supported ratification and argued that a stronger republic could manage conflict and protect the union. **Anti-Federalists** feared concentrated power and the absence of explicit protections for individual liberties. Promises to add a bill of rights helped secure ratification.

:::warning
The Constitution did not abolish slavery or automatically end the international slave trade in 1808. It permitted Congress to prohibit that trade beginning in 1808; Congress then passed legislation to do so.
:::

## The first party system and foreign affairs

### Hamilton and Jefferson

**Alexander Hamilton** proposed federal assumption of state debts, reliable repayment of national debt, and a national bank. He wanted public credit and economic development to strengthen the federal government. **Thomas Jefferson** and **James Madison** feared that these policies favored financiers and concentrated power.

| Federalists | Democratic-Republicans |
| --- | --- |
| Supported Hamilton's financial program | Opposed much of Hamilton's program |
| Favored a stronger national government | Emphasized limited federal authority and agrarian independence |
| Drew support from many merchants and commercial centers | Drew support from many southern and western farmers and planters |
| Generally favored stable relations with Britain | Often sympathized with revolutionary France |

The **Whiskey Rebellion** in 1794 tested federal taxation. Washington mobilized troops to enforce the law, contrasting the new government's capacity with the Confederation's weakness.

### Neutrality and civil liberties

Washington sought neutrality in European war. **Jay's Treaty** eased tensions with Britain but angered critics who thought it conceded too much. **Pinckney's Treaty** with Spain secured navigation of the Mississippi and access to New Orleans. The **Treaty of Greenville**, following the Battle of Fallen Timbers, forced major Native land cessions in the Ohio region.

Under John Adams, the **XYZ Affair** increased hostility toward France and contributed to an undeclared naval conflict. The **Alien and Sedition Acts** restricted immigrants and punished some criticism of the government. The **Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions** challenged these laws and advanced arguments about state resistance to unconstitutional federal action.

Jefferson's victory in **1800** led to a peaceful transfer of power between parties. The election demonstrated the durability of the constitutional system despite fierce disagreement.

## Connections and recap

| Historical connection | Evidence from this period |
| --- | --- |
| Causes of revolution | Postwar taxation, representation disputes, and failed reconciliation |
| Limits of revolutionary change | Continued slavery and unequal citizenship |
| Building federal power | Articles' weaknesses, Constitution, financial program, Whiskey Rebellion |
| Persistent disagreements | State authority, civil liberties, foreign alliances, and economic policy |

:::summary{title="Period 3"}
Imperial reform after 1763 helped turn colonial resistance into independence. Americans then debated how to create a republic strong enough to govern without threatening liberty. The Constitution provided a lasting framework, but disputes over slavery, federal power, and political opposition remained unresolved.
:::
