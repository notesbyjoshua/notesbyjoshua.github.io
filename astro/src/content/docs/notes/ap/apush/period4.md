## Jefferson and the power of the federal government

**Thomas Jefferson** favored an agrarian republic, lower federal spending, and a limited central government. In office, however, he sometimes used broad federal powers when he believed national interests required it.

The **Louisiana Purchase** in 1803 roughly doubled U.S. territory. It secured control of New Orleans and encouraged western settlement. Jefferson questioned whether the Constitution explicitly authorized the purchase but accepted it through the treaty-making process. The **Lewis and Clark expedition** gathered information and built diplomatic contacts while depending heavily on Native knowledge and assistance.

:::key{name="Principles and policy"}
Jefferson's purchase illustrates a recurring pattern: leaders who favor limited government may expand federal action when facing opportunities or crises. Compare stated principles with the choices made in office.
:::

The Supreme Court under **John Marshall** strengthened national institutions:

| Case | Main principle | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marbury v. Madison, 1803 | Judicial review | The Court can declare a federal law unconstitutional |
| McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819 | Implied powers and federal supremacy | Congress can create a national bank; a state cannot tax it in a way that obstructs federal authority |
| Gibbons v. Ogden, 1824 | Federal power over interstate commerce | Federal licensing takes precedence over a conflicting state monopoly |

These decisions did not remove all limits on national power. They clarified that the federal system was more than a loose agreement among fully independent states.

### The election of 1800 and the transfer of power

Jefferson described his election as a political revolution because it transferred power away from the Federalists while preserving constitutional government. He reduced some federal expenditures and allowed the Alien and Sedition Acts' controversy to recede. Yet he retained important parts of the system built during the 1790s, including the national government's responsibility for public credit.

The transfer also produced a conflict over the judiciary. Federalists had made appointments before leaving office, creating disputes over whether the incoming administration would recognize them. In Marbury v. Madison, Marshall asserted the Court's power to review federal laws while avoiding a direct order that Jefferson's administration might refuse to obey. The decision strengthened the judiciary through a carefully limited immediate outcome.

The Louisiana Purchase sharpened tensions between constitutional principles and political goals. Controlling the Mississippi's outlet protected western farmers' access to markets and reduced dependence on a foreign power. The purchase also raised questions about incorporating residents with different languages and legal traditions, governing distant territory, and determining slavery's future.

Exploration helped translate a diplomatic purchase into usable information for officials and settlers. Maps, reports, and scientific observations supported later expansion. They also represented land already inhabited by Native peoples as a field for U.S. development. An expedition could be scientific, diplomatic, and imperial at the same time.

:::key{name="Territory and authority"}
Buying a European empire's claim did not mean Native nations had consented to U.S. rule. The Louisiana Purchase changed relations between states while leaving sovereignty on the ground contested.
:::

## Foreign conflict and national identity

### The War of 1812

British **impressment** of sailors, interference with U.S. trade, and support for Native resistance fueled demands for war. **War Hawks** in Congress also saw war as a chance to defend national honor and possibly gain territory.

Jefferson's **Embargo Act of 1807** tried to pressure Britain and France by stopping U.S. exports. It damaged American commerce and encouraged smuggling without achieving its goals.

The War of 1812 produced mixed military results. Britain burned Washington, while the United States defended Baltimore. The **Treaty of Ghent** restored prewar boundaries. Andrew Jackson's victory at **New Orleans** occurred after the treaty was signed but before news arrived in the United States.

Native confederacies suffered major losses. **Tecumseh** and **Tenskwatawa** had sought to resist U.S. expansion through intertribal cooperation and cultural renewal. Tecumseh's death during the war weakened that resistance in the Old Northwest.

The **Hartford Convention**, where New England Federalists criticized the war, damaged the party's reputation when peace and victory celebrations followed. The resulting **Era of Good Feelings** described the decline of national party competition, not the disappearance of sectional or economic conflict.

### Nationalism after an inconclusive war

The Treaty of Ghent did not produce the territorial gains some War Hawks had hoped for or explicitly resolve every maritime grievance. Nevertheless, many Americans interpreted survival against Britain as a vindication of independence. News of New Orleans reinforced this interpretation and helped make Jackson a national figure.

The war also encouraged domestic manufacturing because interrupted imports created opportunities for American producers. After peace, renewed British competition led some manufacturers to support protective tariffs. Economic nationalism therefore grew partly from the experience of war and the vulnerabilities it revealed.

For Native nations, the result was more damaging. The loss of British military support reduced their ability to resist U.S. expansion. In the South, Jackson's campaigns against the Creeks forced major land cessions, including from communities that had not fought against him. Territorial outcomes cannot be judged solely by whether the United States and Britain exchanged land.

The decline of the Federalists left the Democratic-Republicans dominant nationally, but competing interests remained within that broad party. Disagreements over tariffs, banking, and slavery eventually reorganized political competition. A one-party period could contain the foundations of a new party system.

### Diplomacy after the war

The **Adams-Onís Treaty** transferred Florida from Spain to the United States and established a boundary with Spanish territory. The **Monroe Doctrine** of 1823 opposed new European colonization and intervention in the Americas while declaring U.S. nonintervention in European affairs.

:::warning
The Monroe Doctrine was a policy declaration, not proof that the United States could control the Western Hemisphere in 1823. British naval power and European circumstances helped discourage intervention.
:::

## The market revolution

The **market revolution** connected households and local economies more closely to regional, national, and international markets. More people produced goods for sale and purchased items they had once made themselves.

### Transportation and communication

Roads, canals, steamboats, and railroads reduced the time and cost of moving goods. The **Erie Canal**, completed in 1825, linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and strengthened New York City's commercial position. The telegraph later made long-distance communication much faster.

**Henry Clay's American System** proposed a national bank, protective tariffs, and federally supported internal improvements. Support differed by region because tariffs and transportation projects did not benefit everyone equally.

### The movement from household production to market dependence

Before widespread industrialization, many households combined farming with the production of cloth, tools, and other necessities. Market exchange already existed, but transportation costs limited how far most producers could sell ordinary goods. Better routes increased the number of customers a farmer or manufacturer could reach and encouraged specialization.

The **putting-out system** was an intermediate arrangement in which merchants supplied materials to households that completed work for payment. Factories concentrated workers and machinery in one location, allowing owners to supervise time and pace more closely. The change transferred greater control of production from household workers and artisans to employers and managers.

Transportation improvements could create reinforcing growth. A canal lowered shipping costs, which encouraged settlement and increased production along its route. More traffic then supported warehouses, banks, and towns. But improvements also favored some routes and cities over others, making the location and financing of projects politically contentious.

The expanding economy depended on credit. Banks issued loans and notes that helped farmers, merchants, and businesses invest. Credit supported growth when trade expanded, but a sudden contraction could force borrowers to sell property or close enterprises. The **Panic of 1819** exposed these risks and encouraged suspicion of banks, especially among people who associated foreclosure with distant financial institutions.

:::note
Market growth changed people's exposure to events far away. A farmer could work just as hard and still lose income because prices fell in a distant market or a bank reduced credit.
:::

### Work and social change

Factories reorganized production around machinery and wage labor. At **Lowell**, young women worked in textile mills under closely supervised conditions. Their wages created opportunities, but long hours and deteriorating conditions also prompted protest.

- **Farmers** increasingly specialized in crops for distant markets and became more exposed to price changes and debt.
- **Artisans** faced competition from factories and the breakdown of skilled production into separate tasks.
- **Workers** organized for shorter hours and better conditions, though early unions had limited power.
- **Middle-class families** increasingly promoted separate domestic and public roles for women and men. This ideal did not describe the lives of all working women.

Immigration, especially from Ireland and Germany in the 1840s, supplied labor and expanded cities. Nativists portrayed immigrants, particularly Catholics, as threats to jobs and republican institutions.

:::key{name="Economic interdependence"}
Northern textile production depended on southern cotton, while western farmers supplied food to growing markets. Regional economies became more connected even as their political interests diverged.
:::

### Time, wages, and collective action

Factory employers treated punctuality and regular attendance as essential to keeping machinery productive. Workers accustomed to task-oriented labor faced clocks, bells, and rules controlling the workday. This was a social change as well as a technical one: employers increasingly purchased measured hours of labor rather than a finished product made independently.

Lowell's mill owners initially emphasized supervised boardinghouses and respectable conditions to reassure workers' families. As competition increased, employers sought higher output and lower labor costs. Workers responded with petitions and strikes, challenging the idea that factory employment was simply a temporary opportunity without class conflict.

Early labor organizations sought shorter working days, improved conditions, and greater recognition. Their efforts were limited by unemployment, employer resistance, and divisions among workers. Immigration could supply needed labor while also becoming a source of conflict when employers used newcomers to replace striking workers or when nativists blamed migrants for broader economic pressures.

The language of **separate spheres** assigned paid work and politics to men and domestic moral influence to women. This ideal was especially associated with the growing middle class. Working-class women, enslaved women, and many farm women performed demanding economic labor, making it inaccurate to treat the ideal as a universal description of daily life.

## Cotton, slavery, and the South

The **cotton gin** made it easier to clean short-staple cotton. Combined with textile demand and access to western land, it helped make cotton cultivation highly profitable. The result was an expansion of slavery into the Deep South, not a decline in the need for enslaved labor.

The domestic slave trade forcibly moved people from the Upper South to cotton-growing regions, separating families and communities. Enslaved people created kinship networks, religious traditions, and other forms of support despite enslavers' efforts to control them.

Resistance ranged from slowing work and escaping to organized revolt. **Nat Turner's rebellion** in 1831 intensified white southern fears and led to harsher restrictions on Black education, movement, and assembly.

Most white southerners did not own enslaved people, but wealthy slaveholders dominated much of the region's politics. Racial privilege, aspirations to own land and enslaved labor, and economic dependence helped sustain support for slavery among many nonslaveholders. Other white southerners opposed planter power or remained outside plantation society.

:::warning
Slavery was not separate from the modern economy. Cotton linked enslaved labor to banks, shipping, insurance, and industrial textile production on both sides of the Atlantic.
:::

### The cotton frontier and forced migration

Cotton profits encouraged planters to seek land in Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and beyond. Native removal and federal expansion made that land available to settlers, connecting the growth of slavery to territorial policy. The cotton frontier was therefore produced through both private investment and government action.

The domestic slave trade expanded after Congress prohibited legal importation of enslaved people from abroad beginning in 1808. Ending the international trade did not end demand for enslaved labor. Enslavers in older agricultural regions sold people into newer cotton districts, turning family separation into a central part of the expanding economy.

Planters used violence, supervision, and production demands to increase output. Claims that enslaved people were members of a benevolent household concealed their lack of control over work, movement, and family security. The language of paternalism was a justification offered by enslavers, not a neutral description of the relationship.

### Proslavery arguments and antislavery pressure

As abolitionist criticism grew, defenders increasingly described slavery as a **positive good**, claiming that it protected order and supposedly benefited those enslaved. They contrasted slavery with the insecurity of northern wage labor, ignoring the fundamental difference that wage workers were not legally owned. Religious and racial arguments helped present domination as legitimate.

Antislavery activists challenged these claims through testimony, print, speeches, and organizing. The existence of resistance itself undermines the idea that enslaved people accepted enslavement as benevolent. Escape, covert education, family preservation, and rebellion all reveal goals independent of enslavers' expectations.

The **gag rule** in the House of Representatives blocked discussion of many antislavery petitions. Its significance extended beyond slavery: critics argued that suppressing petitions threatened civil liberties for white citizens as well. Attempts to silence antislavery activity could therefore draw new opponents into the controversy.

## Expanding democracy and Jacksonian politics

### A larger electorate, with clear exclusions

Many states removed property requirements for white male voters. Political parties used newspapers, rallies, conventions, and patronage to mobilize a broader electorate. Women, most Black Americans, and Native peoples remained excluded, and some states tightened restrictions on free Black voting.

The disputed election of **1824** ended with John Quincy Adams chosen by the House of Representatives. Jackson's supporters denounced the result as a **corrupt bargain** after Henry Clay became secretary of state. Jackson won in 1828 by presenting himself as a defender of ordinary white men against entrenched elites.

### Parties and popular political identity

The **second party system** organized competition between Democrats and Whigs. Democrats generally emphasized limited federal economic intervention, opposition to privileged institutions, and the political authority of the white male electorate. Whigs generally supported a more active government role in development and accused Jackson of exercising arbitrary executive power.

Neither party represented only one region or economic class. Each built coalitions with internal disagreements, including supporters in both slave and free states. For a time, competition over banks, tariffs, and leadership helped parties maintain national support despite slavery's growing divisiveness.

Party organization mattered because a larger electorate required new ways of reaching voters. Campaign newspapers, local committees, rallies, and conventions built identification with a party between elections. Politics became a form of popular participation rather than an activity confined to a small circle of officeholders.

Jackson defended **rotation in office** as a way to prevent government positions from becoming the property of an entrenched elite. Critics called the distribution of jobs to supporters the **spoils system**. The same practice could be presented as opening government to ordinary people or as rewarding loyalty at the expense of competence.

### Three conflicts under Jackson

| Conflict | Jackson's position | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank War | Opposed the Second Bank as privileged and constitutionally suspect | Vetoed recharter and removed federal deposits |
| Nullification Crisis | Rejected South Carolina's claim that it could nullify a federal tariff | Combined a threat of force with support for compromise |
| Indian removal | Supported removing Native nations from lands sought by settlers | Federal policy enabled mass displacement and suffering |

The **Indian Removal Act of 1830** authorized removal agreements. In **Worcester v. Georgia**, the Supreme Court rejected Georgia's authority within Cherokee territory, but the ruling did not stop removal. The **Trail of Tears**, largely carried out under Martin Van Buren, forcibly displaced Cherokee people and caused thousands of deaths.

Jackson's opponents formed the **Whig Party**, accusing him of excessive executive power. The **Panic of 1837** followed a combination of unstable credit, banking problems, falling cotton prices, and international pressures; it cannot be explained by a single presidential decision.

:::key{name="The limits of democracy"}
Jacksonian democracy widened political participation for many white men while federal policy dispossessed Native peoples and protected slavery. Political inclusion for one group could accompany exclusion of another.
:::

### Comparing Jackson's uses of power

The Nullification Crisis followed South Carolina's opposition to protective tariffs, which its leaders associated with northern advantage and federal overreach. **John C. Calhoun** argued that a state could declare a federal law void within its borders. Jackson rejected that position because it threatened the authority and survival of the Union.

The **Force Bill** and a compromise tariff combined coercion with negotiation. Jackson did not accept that states could decide for themselves whether federal laws applied, even though he often criticized broad federal economic power. His position distinguished opposition to particular policies from acceptance of a right to destroy national authority.

The Bank War followed a different logic. Jackson treated the national bank as an institution serving privilege and used the veto to make a political and constitutional argument against it. Removing federal deposits weakened the bank and shifted funds to selected state banks. Supporters saw this as resistance to concentrated wealth; opponents saw an executive using national power to attack an institution he disliked.

Removal policy again reveals selective use of power. Jackson's administration supported strong federal action when it advanced white settlement, even while invoking limited government elsewhere. Cherokee leaders used written constitutions, diplomacy, petitions, and courts to defend sovereignty, demonstrating that removal did not result from a failure to understand American institutions. Those strategies were overwhelmed by settler demand and government policy.

:::key{name="Consistency and contradiction"}
Jackson opposed some forms of federal power while vigorously using others. Explain what each policy protected: the Union in nullification, his vision of economic opportunity in the Bank War, and white territorial expansion in removal.
:::

## Religion, reform, and American culture

The **Second Great Awakening** emphasized individual conversion and the possibility of moral improvement. Revivals helped inspire reform movements, although religious beliefs also supported defenses of existing hierarchies.

| Movement | Goals and examples | Limits or disagreements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperance | Reduce or eliminate alcohol consumption | Often imposed middle-class Protestant standards on others |
| Education | Horace Mann supported tax-funded common schools | Access and quality remained unequal |
| Prison and asylum reform | Dorothea Dix sought improved treatment of people with mental illness | Institutional care could still be coercive or inadequate |
| Abolition | William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass, and others demanded an end to slavery | Activists disagreed over politics, tactics, and women's participation |
| Women's rights | Seneca Falls and the Declaration of Sentiments demanded legal and political equality | Voting rights remained unrealized in this period |
| Utopian communities | Groups such as the Shakers experimented with communal life | Communities varied widely in religion, property, and gender roles |

Black abolitionists drew on lived experience, churches, newspapers, and organizing networks. **David Walker** attacked slavery and racial oppression, while **Frederick Douglass** used speeches and autobiography to challenge claims that enslaved people accepted their condition.

**Transcendentalists**, including Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, emphasized individual conscience and spiritual insight. Literature and landscape painting, including the **Hudson River School**, helped construct a distinct national culture, often celebrating land that was also the site of Native dispossession.

### Revival and the belief that society could improve

Revivalists such as **Charles Grandison Finney** emphasized people's ability to choose conversion and change their conduct. The idea that individuals could be morally transformed encouraged efforts to transform communities as well. Reformers attacked alcohol abuse, inadequate education, poverty, and institutions they considered degrading.

Religious reform was not the only source of change. Urban growth exposed social problems, expanding print made campaigns easier to organize, and market development supplied both resources and anxieties. Reformers often sought to create order in a society they believed was changing too rapidly.

Temperance illustrates the mixture of motives. Advocates linked alcohol abuse to family poverty and domestic violence, concerns that gave women an important reason to participate. Campaigns could also stigmatize immigrant drinking customs and impose Protestant middle-class expectations. A reform could address a real harm while producing new forms of social control.

### Abolition and women's rights

Abolitionists differed over strategy. Some favored moral persuasion, others political parties or direct assistance to fugitives. **Colonization** proposed removing free Black people from the United States, but many Black activists rejected it because they claimed the country as their own and opposed the assumption that freedom required exile.

Women's participation in antislavery organizing developed skills in petitioning, public speaking, and institution-building. Restrictions within the movement encouraged some activists to question women's broader legal position. **Lucretia Mott** and **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** helped organize Seneca Falls, where the Declaration of Sentiments connected existing rights language to demands for women's equality.

The movement did not represent every woman's experience equally. Black women confronted racial exclusion as well as gender barriers, while economic conditions shaped who had time and resources to organize. Different reform movements overlapped, but their participants could disagree sharply about priorities and tactics.

### Culture, conscience, and belonging

Transcendentalists emphasized the individual's capacity to recognize moral truth, making conscience a potential basis for resisting unjust institutions. Thoreau's criticism of slavery and the Mexican-American War linked this philosophy to political conduct. His ideas about civil disobedience treated obedience as a moral question rather than automatically equating law with justice.

The emergence of **Mormonism** and the migration of its followers also showed the limits of religious toleration. New religious communities could face violence and exclusion despite national commitments to liberty of conscience. Their movement west connected religious experimentation to the broader history of migration and settlement.

## Sectional tensions and the Missouri Compromise

Westward expansion raised the question of whether new states would permit slavery. The **Missouri Compromise of 1820** admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, preserving the Senate balance. It prohibited slavery in the remaining Louisiana Purchase territory north of latitude 36°30′, except Missouri.

The compromise managed an immediate dispute without resolving whether Congress could or should limit slavery's expansion. Economic growth and national pride therefore coexisted with a deepening sectional conflict.
