## Jefferson and the power of the federal government

**Thomas Jefferson** favored an agrarian republic, lower federal spending, and a limited central government. In office, however, he sometimes used broad federal powers when he believed national interests required it.

The **Louisiana Purchase** in 1803 roughly doubled U.S. territory. It secured control of New Orleans and encouraged western settlement. Jefferson questioned whether the Constitution explicitly authorized the purchase but accepted it through the treaty-making process. The **Lewis and Clark expedition** gathered information and built diplomatic contacts while depending heavily on Native knowledge and assistance.

:::key{name="Principles and policy"}
Jefferson's purchase illustrates a recurring pattern: leaders who favor limited government may expand federal action when facing opportunities or crises. Compare stated principles with the choices made in office.
:::

The Supreme Court under **John Marshall** strengthened national institutions:

| Case | Main principle | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Marbury v. Madison, 1803 | Judicial review | The Court can declare a federal law unconstitutional |
| McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819 | Implied powers and federal supremacy | Congress can create a national bank; a state cannot tax it in a way that obstructs federal authority |
| Gibbons v. Ogden, 1824 | Federal power over interstate commerce | Federal licensing takes precedence over a conflicting state monopoly |

These decisions did not remove all limits on national power. They clarified that the federal system was more than a loose agreement among fully independent states.

## Foreign conflict and national identity

### The War of 1812

British **impressment** of sailors, interference with U.S. trade, and support for Native resistance fueled demands for war. **War Hawks** in Congress also saw war as a chance to defend national honor and possibly gain territory.

Jefferson's **Embargo Act of 1807** tried to pressure Britain and France by stopping U.S. exports. It damaged American commerce and encouraged smuggling without achieving its goals.

The War of 1812 produced mixed military results. Britain burned Washington, while the United States defended Baltimore. The **Treaty of Ghent** restored prewar boundaries. Andrew Jackson's victory at **New Orleans** occurred after the treaty was signed but before news arrived in the United States.

Native confederacies suffered major losses. **Tecumseh** and **Tenskwatawa** had sought to resist U.S. expansion through intertribal cooperation and cultural renewal. Tecumseh's death during the war weakened that resistance in the Old Northwest.

The **Hartford Convention**, where New England Federalists criticized the war, damaged the party's reputation when peace and victory celebrations followed. The resulting **Era of Good Feelings** described the decline of national party competition, not the disappearance of sectional or economic conflict.

### Diplomacy after the war

The **Adams-Onís Treaty** transferred Florida from Spain to the United States and established a boundary with Spanish territory. The **Monroe Doctrine** of 1823 opposed new European colonization and intervention in the Americas while declaring U.S. nonintervention in European affairs.

:::warning
The Monroe Doctrine was a policy declaration, not proof that the United States could control the Western Hemisphere in 1823. British naval power and European circumstances helped discourage intervention.
:::

## The market revolution

The **market revolution** connected households and local economies more closely to regional, national, and international markets. More people produced goods for sale and purchased items they had once made themselves.

### Transportation and communication

Roads, canals, steamboats, and railroads reduced the time and cost of moving goods. The **Erie Canal**, completed in 1825, linked the Great Lakes to the Hudson River and strengthened New York City's commercial position. The telegraph later made long-distance communication much faster.

**Henry Clay's American System** proposed a national bank, protective tariffs, and federally supported internal improvements. Support differed by region because tariffs and transportation projects did not benefit everyone equally.

### Work and social change

Factories reorganized production around machinery and wage labor. At **Lowell**, young women worked in textile mills under closely supervised conditions. Their wages created opportunities, but long hours and deteriorating conditions also prompted protest.

- **Farmers** increasingly specialized in crops for distant markets and became more exposed to price changes and debt.
- **Artisans** faced competition from factories and the breakdown of skilled production into separate tasks.
- **Workers** organized for shorter hours and better conditions, though early unions had limited power.
- **Middle-class families** increasingly promoted separate domestic and public roles for women and men. This ideal did not describe the lives of all working women.

Immigration, especially from Ireland and Germany in the 1840s, supplied labor and expanded cities. Nativists portrayed immigrants, particularly Catholics, as threats to jobs and republican institutions.

:::key{name="Economic interdependence"}
Northern textile production depended on southern cotton, while western farmers supplied food to growing markets. Regional economies became more connected even as their political interests diverged.
:::

## Cotton, slavery, and the South

The **cotton gin** made it easier to clean short-staple cotton. Combined with textile demand and access to western land, it helped make cotton cultivation highly profitable. The result was an expansion of slavery into the Deep South, not a decline in the need for enslaved labor.

The domestic slave trade forcibly moved people from the Upper South to cotton-growing regions, separating families and communities. Enslaved people created kinship networks, religious traditions, and other forms of support despite enslavers' efforts to control them.

Resistance ranged from slowing work and escaping to organized revolt. **Nat Turner's rebellion** in 1831 intensified white southern fears and led to harsher restrictions on Black education, movement, and assembly.

Most white southerners did not own enslaved people, but wealthy slaveholders dominated much of the region's politics. Racial privilege, aspirations to own land and enslaved labor, and economic dependence helped sustain support for slavery among many nonslaveholders. Other white southerners opposed planter power or remained outside plantation society.

:::warning
Slavery was not separate from the modern economy. Cotton linked enslaved labor to banks, shipping, insurance, and industrial textile production on both sides of the Atlantic.
:::

## Expanding democracy and Jacksonian politics

### A larger electorate, with clear exclusions

Many states removed property requirements for white male voters. Political parties used newspapers, rallies, conventions, and patronage to mobilize a broader electorate. Women, most Black Americans, and Native peoples remained excluded, and some states tightened restrictions on free Black voting.

The disputed election of **1824** ended with John Quincy Adams chosen by the House of Representatives. Jackson's supporters denounced the result as a **corrupt bargain** after Henry Clay became secretary of state. Jackson won in 1828 by presenting himself as a defender of ordinary white men against entrenched elites.

### Three conflicts under Jackson

| Conflict | Jackson's position | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bank War | Opposed the Second Bank as privileged and constitutionally suspect | Vetoed recharter and removed federal deposits |
| Nullification Crisis | Rejected South Carolina's claim that it could nullify a federal tariff | Combined a threat of force with support for compromise |
| Indian removal | Supported removing Native nations from lands sought by settlers | Federal policy enabled mass displacement and suffering |

The **Indian Removal Act of 1830** authorized removal agreements. In **Worcester v. Georgia**, the Supreme Court rejected Georgia's authority within Cherokee territory, but the ruling did not stop removal. The **Trail of Tears**, largely carried out under Martin Van Buren, forcibly displaced Cherokee people and caused thousands of deaths.

Jackson's opponents formed the **Whig Party**, accusing him of excessive executive power. The **Panic of 1837** followed a combination of unstable credit, banking problems, falling cotton prices, and international pressures; it cannot be explained by a single presidential decision.

:::key{name="The limits of democracy"}
Jacksonian democracy widened political participation for many white men while federal policy dispossessed Native peoples and protected slavery. Political inclusion for one group could accompany exclusion of another.
:::

## Religion, reform, and American culture

The **Second Great Awakening** emphasized individual conversion and the possibility of moral improvement. Revivals helped inspire reform movements, although religious beliefs also supported defenses of existing hierarchies.

| Movement | Goals and examples | Limits or disagreements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperance | Reduce or eliminate alcohol consumption | Often imposed middle-class Protestant standards on others |
| Education | Horace Mann supported tax-funded common schools | Access and quality remained unequal |
| Prison and asylum reform | Dorothea Dix sought improved treatment of people with mental illness | Institutional care could still be coercive or inadequate |
| Abolition | William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass, and others demanded an end to slavery | Activists disagreed over politics, tactics, and women's participation |
| Women's rights | Seneca Falls and the Declaration of Sentiments demanded legal and political equality | Voting rights remained unrealized in this period |
| Utopian communities | Groups such as the Shakers experimented with communal life | Communities varied widely in religion, property, and gender roles |

Black abolitionists drew on lived experience, churches, newspapers, and organizing networks. **David Walker** attacked slavery and racial oppression, while **Frederick Douglass** used speeches and autobiography to challenge claims that enslaved people accepted their condition.

**Transcendentalists**, including Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau, emphasized individual conscience and spiritual insight. Literature and landscape painting, including the **Hudson River School**, helped construct a distinct national culture, often celebrating land that was also the site of Native dispossession.

## Sectional tensions and the Missouri Compromise

Westward expansion raised the question of whether new states would permit slavery. The **Missouri Compromise of 1820** admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, preserving the Senate balance. It prohibited slavery in the remaining Louisiana Purchase territory north of latitude 36°30′, except Missouri.

The compromise managed an immediate dispute without resolving whether Congress could or should limit slavery's expansion. Economic growth and national pride therefore coexisted with a deepening sectional conflict.

## Connections and recap

| Historical connection | Evidence from this period |
| --- | --- |
| Federal power and its critics | Marshall Court, national bank, nullification |
| Economic transformation | Canals, factories, wage labor, and expanding cotton slavery |
| Inclusion and exclusion | White male suffrage alongside removal and racial restrictions |
| Reform and national culture | Religious revivals, abolition, women's rights, and transcendentalism |

:::summary{title="Period 4"}
Territorial growth and the market revolution transformed the economy and daily life. Mass politics expanded participation for white men, while slavery and removal denied freedom to others. Reform movements challenged these inequalities, and disputes over western slavery exposed the limits of national unity.
:::
