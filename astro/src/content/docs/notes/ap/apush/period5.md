## Manifest Destiny and westward expansion

**Manifest Destiny** was the belief that the United States was destined to expand across the continent. Supporters connected expansion to opportunity, republican government, and national power. The idea also drew on racial assumptions that justified taking land from Native peoples and Mexicans.

Expansion had several causes: demand for farmland, commercial opportunities in the Pacific, population movement, and political ambitions. It was contested because new territory raised questions about slavery and the use of federal power.

### Texas, Oregon, and war with Mexico

American settlers in Mexican Texas resisted Mexican authority, including restrictions affecting slavery. Texas won independence in 1836, but annexation remained controversial. The United States annexed Texas in 1845, and a boundary dispute between the Nueces River and Rio Grande contributed to war with Mexico.

President **James K. Polk** also pursued Oregon. A treaty with Britain in 1846 established the boundary at the 49th parallel across most of the disputed region, avoiding another war.

The **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** ended the Mexican-American War in 1848. Mexico recognized the Rio Grande boundary and ceded a vast western territory. The later **Gadsden Purchase** acquired land useful for a possible southern railroad route.

The **California gold rush** drew migrants from across the United States and abroad. It accelerated statehood, commercial growth, and violence against Native peoples. Mexican residents often faced dispossession despite treaty protections, while Chinese migrants encountered discrimination and exclusionary taxes.

:::warning
Manifest Destiny describes an ideology used to support expansion. It does not mean expansion was inevitable, peaceful, or accepted by the people already living in the territory.
:::

### Expansion as policy rather than destiny

Polk's election in 1844 gave expansionists a president committed to territorial growth, but Americans disagreed about the purposes and limits of that growth. Northern critics suspected that annexing Texas would strengthen slaveholding interests. Others supported expansion because they wanted access to Pacific commerce or new farms without necessarily supporting the spread of slavery.

The war with Mexico raised constitutional questions about executive authority. Polk claimed that Mexico had attacked Americans on American soil, but the fighting occurred in disputed territory. **Abraham Lincoln's Spot Resolutions** challenged the administration to identify the exact location and justify its claim. Opposition to the war could therefore combine antislavery concerns with criticism of presidential power.

Settlers' movement also depended on infrastructure, information, and public policy. Overland trails connected migrants to western destinations, but travel was dangerous and expensive. Traders, military posts, guides, and prior migrants helped make movement possible. The image of completely self-sufficient pioneers obscures these supporting networks.

### Who bore the costs of expansion?

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo changed national boundaries without moving every person who lived within them. Mexican residents of ceded territory became a minority within an expanding state, and formal promises of property protection often failed to prevent land loss. Legal costs, disputed titles, discrimination, and settler pressure weakened their position.

In California, miners and settlers disrupted Native access to food and land, while forced labor and violence devastated communities. The gold rush was therefore a source of commercial opportunity for some and catastrophe for others. Chinese migrants worked in mining and other occupations while facing discriminatory restrictions and attacks.

These developments also changed the national political timetable. California's rapid population growth and application for statehood forced Congress to confront slavery before a stable territorial arrangement had developed. Migration turned an abstract disagreement about future territory into an immediate question about representation.

:::key{name="Expansion and sectionalism"}
Territorial acquisition did not automatically cause civil war. It created repeated decisions about slavery, citizenship, and political power that existing institutions became less able to settle.
:::

## Why western land destabilized national politics

The central dispute was whether slavery would expand into new territories. Expansion affected not only labor systems but also the future balance of power in Congress.

**Free-soil** supporters opposed the spread of slavery into western territories, often because they wanted opportunities for free white labor. Some were abolitionists, but opposition to slavery's expansion did not necessarily include support for racial equality or immediate abolition where slavery already existed.

The **Wilmot Proviso** proposed banning slavery in land acquired from Mexico. It did not become law, but the debate exposed sectional divisions that cut across existing parties.

### Four positions on territorial slavery

Debate involved more than two perfectly united sections. **Abolitionists** demanded an end to slavery itself. **Free-soilers** sought to prevent its expansion, often to protect opportunities for white workers. **Popular sovereignty** offered territorial settlers a decision, while many southern leaders insisted that slaveholders had a constitutional right to bring enslaved property into federal territories.

Each position implied a different role for Congress. A congressional ban treated slavery as something the national legislature could restrict. Popular sovereignty tried to shift responsibility away from Congress, but it left questions about when settlers could decide and whether their decision would remain binding. The demand for federal protection of slavery required national intervention on behalf of slaveholders.

Southern politicians therefore did not consistently favor a weak federal government. They demanded federal enforcement of fugitive-slave obligations and increasingly sought federal protection for slavery in the territories. Northern politicians likewise differed over whether national power should protect liberty or preserve sectional compromise.

The **Free Soil Party** brought some antislavery Democrats, former Whigs, and abolitionists together in 1848. Its coalition demonstrated that resistance to slavery's expansion could reorganize politics, even when its members disagreed about Black equality. The later Republican coalition inherited some of these tensions.

### The Compromise of 1850

| Provision | Political significance |
| --- | --- |
| California enters as a free state | Free states gain an advantage in the Senate |
| Utah and New Mexico organize without an immediate federal slavery ban | Leaves the issue to later territorial and state decisions |
| A stronger Fugitive Slave Act | Requires greater cooperation in capturing people accused of escaping slavery |
| Slave trade ends in Washington, D.C. | Ends the trade there, but not slavery itself |
| Texas boundary and debt settlement | Resolves a territorial dispute |

The **Fugitive Slave Act** denied alleged fugitives basic procedural protections and threatened free Black people with kidnapping. Northern resistance, personal liberty laws, and escape networks increased southern complaints that the North would not honor constitutional obligations. **Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin** brought antislavery arguments to a large audience.

:::key{name="Compromise and conflict"}
The Compromise of 1850 postponed a sectional break, but enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act brought slavery directly into northern communities. A measure designed to settle conflict also created new resistance.
:::

### Why the Fugitive Slave Act changed northern politics

The law required national cooperation with slavery's enforcement. Federal commissioners handled cases, and alleged fugitives faced procedures that denied safeguards available in ordinary criminal trials. Free Black residents could also be endangered by fraudulent claims, making the issue one of personal security throughout Black communities.

For white northerners who had previously regarded slavery as a distant southern institution, enforcement made the question local. They might be asked to assist capture or witness the forced return of someone living in their town. Personal liberty laws, legal defense efforts, and rescue attempts arose from this confrontation.

Southern slaveholders viewed obstruction as evidence that the North would not respect their property claims or constitutional rights. Thus an enforcement measure strengthened northern resistance and southern distrust at the same time. The compromise stabilized congressional bargaining temporarily while worsening relationships outside Congress.

**Harriet Tubman** and others assisted escape through networks later grouped under the name **Underground Railroad**. These were varied local arrangements, not one centralized railway-like organization. Enslaved people themselves made the dangerous decision to flee, and their actions forced the conflict over freedom into courts, communities, and interstate politics.

## The collapse of compromise

### Kansas-Nebraska and party realignment

The **Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854**, promoted by Stephen Douglas, used **popular sovereignty** to let settlers decide the slavery question. It effectively repealed the Missouri Compromise restriction in those territories.

Proslavery and antislavery settlers fought for control of Kansas. Fraud, intimidation, and violence produced **Bleeding Kansas**. Popular sovereignty did not resolve the dispute because both sides considered the outcome too important to accept defeat.

The Whig Party collapsed under sectional pressure. The new **Republican Party** opposed slavery's expansion, while the **Know-Nothing Party** emphasized anti-immigrant and anti-Catholic politics. Slavery increasingly displaced older disputes as the central organizing issue in national politics.

### Violence and the breakdown of a national party system

The Kansas-Nebraska Act mattered because it reversed an arrangement many northerners had considered settled. Even people willing to tolerate slavery where it existed could oppose reopening territory previously closed to it. The controversy made southern demands appear expansionist rather than merely defensive.

Competing migration campaigns sought to shape Kansas's electorate. Proslavery border-crossers and antislavery settlers challenged the legitimacy of one another's governments, and violence made a peaceful local vote increasingly unrealistic. The controversy over the proslavery **Lecompton Constitution** later divided Democrats, including President Buchanan and Stephen Douglas.

Violence also reached Congress. The **caning of Charles Sumner** after an antislavery speech became a symbol of sectional hostility. Northerners and southerners interpreted the incident through competing ideas of honor, insult, and political intimidation, further narrowing the space for compromise.

The Whigs had previously joined people across sections around economic development and opposition to Democratic leadership. Slavery made that coalition harder to sustain because voters increasingly evaluated national issues through sectional loyalties. Republican strength was concentrated in the free states, making its success alarming to southerners accustomed to influence within national parties.

### Dred Scott, John Brown, and the election of 1860

In **Dred Scott v. Sandford** in 1857, the Supreme Court held that Black people of African descent were not citizens for purposes of federal citizenship and that Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories. The decision undermined both the Missouri Compromise and the idea that Congress could contain slavery.

The **Lincoln-Douglas debates** in 1858 centered on slavery's expansion. Lincoln opposed expansion but did not yet advocate immediate federal abolition in the states. Douglas defended popular sovereignty, angering some southerners by suggesting local authorities could effectively exclude slavery by withholding protective laws.

**John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry** in 1859 attempted to initiate an uprising against slavery. Its failure did not prevent it from deepening southern fears of northern hostility.

Lincoln won the **1860 election** with a divided opposition and without carrying a slave state. South Carolina seceded, followed by other Deep South states. Their leaders identified the protection of slavery as central to their decision. The attack on **Fort Sumter** and Lincoln's call for troops brought additional states into the Confederacy.

:::warning
“States' rights” is incomplete as an explanation of secession unless you identify the right being defended. Secessionist leaders sought to preserve slavery and its political protection.
:::

### Secession and the failure of last-minute settlement

Lincoln's election showed that a candidate could win the presidency without carrying the South. Although he promised not to abolish slavery directly in states where it already existed, southern secessionists feared that restricting its expansion would place the institution on a path toward eventual decline. Their concern was about its future security as well as immediate policy.

Compromise proposals attempted to guarantee slavery or extend earlier geographic arrangements. Republicans resisted concessions that would abandon their central commitment against expansion. The disagreement was not simply a failure of politeness: the parties were being asked to surrender goals their supporters considered fundamental.

Lincoln denied that a state could unilaterally leave the Union. The conflict at Fort Sumter turned the constitutional dispute into open warfare. After his call for troops, additional Upper South states seceded, while border slave states remained in the Union. Their presence mattered strategically and helps explain why early Union policy separated preserving the Union from universal emancipation.

:::warning
The Union and the free states were not identical. Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the Union while permitting slavery at the start of the war. This complicates any claim that the conflict initially divided neatly into a wholly free North and wholly enslaved South.
:::

## The Civil War

### Resources and strategy

The Union had a larger population, more industry, a stronger railroad network, and greater naval capacity. The Confederacy hoped to defend its territory long enough to exhaust northern support and gain foreign recognition.

Union strategy combined a naval blockade, control of the Mississippi River, and attacks on Confederate armies. Confederate hopes for European intervention rested partly on cotton demand, but foreign governments never formally recognized Confederate independence.

| Turning point | Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Antietam, 1862 | Checked a Confederate invasion and gave Lincoln an opportunity to announce emancipation |
| Gettysburg, 1863 | Ended Lee's major invasion of Pennsylvania |
| Vicksburg, 1863 | Gave the Union control of the Mississippi and divided the Confederacy |
| Sherman's campaigns, 1864 | Attacked infrastructure and resources supporting the Confederate war effort |
| Appomattox, 1865 | Lee surrendered his army; other Confederate forces surrendered afterward |

### Turning resources into victory

The Union's advantages required organization and political endurance. Factories had to supply armies, railroads had to move troops and food, and commanders had to coordinate campaigns. Superior resources made sustained warfare possible but did not guarantee rapid success, as early defeats and high casualties demonstrated.

The Confederacy could fight defensively and hoped that northern voters would reject continued war. Its large territory made occupation difficult, but defending that territory also strained manpower and transportation. Blockade, shortages, inflation, and damage to railroads weakened its ability to keep armies supplied.

Enslaved labor supported the Confederate economy and allowed more white men to serve in the army. At the same time, enslaved people who escaped deprived the Confederacy of labor and supplied the Union with information and recruits. Slavery was both a resource the Confederacy sought to defend and a vulnerability people acted against from within.

The election of **1864** became a test of whether the Union would continue fighting. Military successes, including the capture of Atlanta, helped Lincoln win reelection. Union victory therefore depended on maintaining political support as well as defeating Confederate armies.

### Hard war and civilian resources

Sherman's campaigns targeted railroads, supplies, and property supporting the war effort. The purpose was to reduce the Confederacy's material capacity and willingness to continue. Civilians experienced destruction and displacement even when military operations did not aim to kill them directly.

The phrase **total war** should be used with care. Civil War armies increasingly attacked economic resources and civilian morale, but their methods and scale differed from twentieth-century industrial warfare. The useful comparison concerns the widening range of legitimate military targets, not an assumption that the wars were identical.

### Emancipation and Black military service

Lincoln initially presented the war as a struggle to preserve the Union. Enslaved people who escaped to Union lines forced military and political leaders to confront slavery directly. Congressional confiscation measures and antislavery pressure also pushed policy toward emancipation.

The **Emancipation Proclamation**, effective January 1, 1863, declared freedom for enslaved people in designated areas in rebellion. It did not immediately free everyone: it excluded loyal slave states and some Union-controlled areas. Its enforcement depended on Union military success.

Black soldiers served in the Union Army and Navy, strengthening the war effort and making claims to citizenship. They faced unequal treatment and particular danger if captured by Confederate forces.

:::key{name="Emancipation and abolition"}
The Emancipation Proclamation used wartime executive authority against slavery in rebellious areas. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery nationwide, except as punishment for crime after conviction.
:::

### Emancipation as a process

The movement from preserving the Union to destroying slavery involved Congress, the army, the president, abolitionists, and enslaved people. People escaping to Union lines created practical questions about whether soldiers should return them. Treating escapees as **contraband of war** became one early way to deny enslavers' claims without initially declaring universal abolition.

The proclamation made emancipation an explicit Union war aim and authorized Black enlistment. It also made European support for the Confederacy politically more difficult because intervention would now more openly support a slaveholding power against a war for freedom.

Freedom still depended on military reach and information. An order issued in Washington did not instantly remove an enslaver's control in a place beyond Union occupation. The experience remembered through **Juneteenth** illustrates the gap between national proclamation and local enforcement.

Black military service strengthened arguments for equal citizenship: people who fought to preserve the nation could demand recognition within it. Yet unequal pay, discrimination, and Confederate threats against captured Black soldiers revealed how far military participation remained from full equality. The war created powerful claims to rights that Reconstruction would have to address.

### Government and the home front

Both sides used conscription and expanded government power. Draft resistance exposed class and racial tensions, including the New York City draft riots. Lincoln's suspension of habeas corpus raised questions about civil liberties during emergencies.

Republicans also passed the **Homestead Act**, **Morrill Land-Grant Act**, and railroad legislation. These measures supported western settlement, higher education, and economic development while facilitating further Native dispossession. Women worked as nurses, organizers, agricultural laborers, and industrial workers.

## Reconstruction: rebuilding the Union and defining freedom

After Lincoln's assassination, **Andrew Johnson** favored rapid restoration of southern states with limited protections for formerly enslaved people. Southern **Black Codes** restricted Black mobility, labor choices, and legal rights. Congressional Republicans responded by taking a larger role in Reconstruction.

| Amendment | Main change | Limits in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thirteenth, 1865 | Abolishes slavery, with a punishment-for-crime exception | Coercive labor systems continue through discriminatory criminal enforcement |
| Fourteenth, 1868 | Establishes birthright citizenship and equal protection; protects due process | Enforcement depends on courts and federal action |
| Fifteenth, 1870 | Prohibits voting discrimination based on race, color, or previous servitude | Does not prohibit every voting restriction or establish women's suffrage |

The **Reconstruction Acts** placed much of the former Confederacy under military administration and required new constitutions and political changes for readmission. Johnson resisted congressional policy and was impeached, but the Senate fell one vote short of removing him.

The **Freedmen's Bureau** assisted with education, labor disputes, and relief. Black Americans built schools, churches, and political organizations, reunited families, and held public office. Reconstruction governments expanded public education and other services despite limited resources and fierce opposition.

### Presidential and congressional Reconstruction

Lincoln's wartime reconstruction proposals emphasized restoring loyal governments quickly. Johnson's later approach allowed many former Confederates to recover political influence and property other than enslaved people. Southern attempts to restrict freedpeople convinced many congressional Republicans that reunion without stronger conditions would preserve much of the old order.

The **Civil Rights Act of 1866** asserted citizenship and basic legal rights. Congress overrode Johnson's veto, demonstrating a break between presidential and congressional policy. Republicans then sought constitutional protection through the Fourteenth Amendment because ordinary legislation could be weakened or reversed by future governments.

**Radical Republicans** favored stronger protections and a more thorough transformation of southern society. Other Republicans supported measures they considered necessary to secure the Union victory without endorsing every radical proposal. Congressional Reconstruction emerged through bargaining within the Republican Party as well as conflict with Johnson.

Johnson's impeachment centered formally on his removal of Secretary of War Edwin Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act, but the deeper conflict concerned control of Reconstruction. His acquittal did not restore his ability to direct the process as he had earlier.

### What freedom meant to formerly enslaved people

Freedpeople sought control over family life, movement, education, work, and religion. Legal marriage could protect relationships slavery had refused to recognize. Independent churches provided places for worship, schooling, mutual aid, and political organizing. Education mattered both for opportunity and for protection against exploitation in contracts and public life.

Political participation was equally concrete. Black voters helped elect state governments, and Black officeholders served at local, state, and national levels. Reconstruction was not simply a policy imposed on the South by outsiders; formerly enslaved people actively shaped its institutions and priorities.

Land was central because economic independence could make legal freedom more secure. Without land or reliable access to credit, workers often had to bargain with the same landowners who had previously enslaved them. That imbalance helps explain why emancipation transformed legal status without distributing economic power equally.

### Land and labor

Most freedpeople did not receive land. Under **sharecropping**, families farmed land owned by someone else in exchange for a share of the crop. The **crop-lien system** tied borrowers to merchants through credit secured by future harvests. Debt, discriminatory enforcement, and dependence on cotton restricted economic independence.

:::warning
Sharecropping was not legally identical to slavery: freedpeople could marry, build institutions, and negotiate in ways slavery had denied. But debt and violence sharply limited the freedom promised by emancipation.
:::

## Resistance to Reconstruction and federal retreat

White supremacist groups, including the **Ku Klux Klan**, used terror to suppress Black voting and overthrow Republican governments. Federal **Enforcement Acts** temporarily strengthened the response, but sustained protection weakened over time.

Economic crisis after the **Panic of 1873**, northern racism, political fatigue, court decisions narrowing federal protections, and Democratic electoral gains all undermined Reconstruction. The disputed election of 1876 ended with **Rutherford B. Hayes** becoming president and the withdrawal of remaining federal troops guarding Republican state governments in 1877.

This settlement, often called the **Compromise of 1877**, marked a major federal retreat. It did not immediately create every later Jim Crow law; systematic disfranchisement and segregation expanded over the following decades. The Reconstruction amendments remained part of the Constitution and later provided foundations for civil rights struggles.

### Why Reconstruction's achievements endured unevenly

Federal enforcement could suppress some white supremacist violence, but protection depended on sustained political commitment. When officials withdrew or courts restricted federal reach, local power often returned to people determined to restore white rule. The problem was not simply that constitutional language was absent; the institutions responsible for enforcing it were weakened.

Southern Democrats described themselves as **Redeemers**, claiming to rescue their states from Republican rule. Their victories involved a combination of electoral organizing, intimidation, fraud, and violence. The label expresses their political claim rather than a neutral judgment that Reconstruction governments needed redemption.

The amendments nevertheless changed the constitutional order permanently. Later civil rights activists could appeal to national citizenship and equal protection in ways unavailable before the war. Reconstruction therefore combined a major expansion of rights with a failure to secure those rights consistently in everyday life.

The period's central sequence is territorial expansion, sectional breakdown, war, emancipation, and a struggle over the meaning of freedom. Each stage changed the questions facing the next. Winning independence from Britain had left slavery intact; winning the Civil War ended slavery but left the distribution of land, safety, and political power unresolved.
