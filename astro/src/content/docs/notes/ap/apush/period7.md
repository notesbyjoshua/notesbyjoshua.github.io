## Imperialism and America's role abroad

Industrial growth encouraged interest in foreign markets, investment, naval bases, and strategic routes. **Alfred Thayer Mahan** argued that sea power was essential to national strength. Imperialists also used racial and religious claims to justify ruling other peoples.

The **Spanish-American War** of 1898 grew from Cuba's struggle against Spanish rule, U.S. economic interests, sensational reporting, and the explosion of the battleship **Maine**. The cause of the explosion was uncertain, but public pressure for war increased.

Spain's defeat brought Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines under U.S. control. Cuba became formally independent, but the **Platt Amendment** gave the United States extensive intervention rights and access to a naval base at Guantánamo Bay. The United States also annexed Hawaii in 1898, following the overthrow of Queen Liliʻuokalani by interests backed by U.S. involvement in 1893.

Filipino nationalists who had fought Spain resisted U.S. rule in the **Philippine-American War**. Anti-imperialists argued that colonial rule contradicted government by consent. Other opponents feared economic competition or racial incorporation, so opposition to empire did not always rest on equal-rights principles.

:::key{name="Expansion and citizenship"}
Overseas expansion raised a different problem from simply admitting new states: could the United States govern people indefinitely without giving them equal political membership? The Insular Cases allowed unequal constitutional treatment of certain territories.
:::

### Different approaches to intervention

| Policy | Main approach |
| --- | --- |
| Open Door policy | Seek equal commercial access to China and oppose its division into exclusive foreign spheres |
| Roosevelt Corollary | Claim a U.S. role in intervening in Latin American countries to prevent European intervention |
| Dollar Diplomacy | Use investment and financial influence to advance U.S. interests |
| Wilson's diplomacy | Emphasize moral principles while still using military intervention, including in Mexico and the Caribbean |

The **Panama Canal** strengthened naval and commercial connections between the Atlantic and Pacific. Its construction followed U.S. support for Panama's separation from Colombia and an agreement giving the United States control of the canal zone.

## Progressivism

**Progressives** sought to address corruption, unsafe work, urban poverty, corporate abuses, and other problems associated with industrialization. They were not a single movement. Some emphasized democracy, others expert administration, moral reform, or social control.

### Reform from local to national government

**Muckrakers** exposed abuses through investigative journalism. Upton Sinclair's **The Jungle** helped build support for meat inspection and food regulation. Settlement-house workers connected direct services with demands for housing, labor, and public-health reform.

| Reform | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Initiative, referendum, and recall | Give voters more direct influence over legislation and officeholders |
| Direct primaries | Reduce party leaders' control over nominations |
| Sixteenth Amendment | Permit a federal income tax without apportionment among states |
| Seventeenth Amendment | Establish direct election of U.S. senators |
| Eighteenth Amendment | Prohibit the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquor |
| Nineteenth Amendment | Prohibit denial of voting rights on the basis of sex |

**Theodore Roosevelt's Square Deal** supported regulation, consumer protection, and conservation. He distinguished between corporations he considered useful and those abusing their power. **William Howard Taft** pursued antitrust enforcement but split with Roosevelt over policy and party leadership. In 1912, their division helped **Woodrow Wilson** win the presidency.

Wilson's reforms included the **Federal Reserve Act**, **Federal Trade Commission**, and **Clayton Antitrust Act**. Conservation policies sought managed use of resources, while preservationists such as **John Muir** wanted stronger protection from development.

### The limits of reform

Many Progressives ignored racial inequality or supported segregation and coercive policies. Wilson's administration segregated federal workplaces. **W. E. B. Du Bois** and the **NAACP** pressed for civil rights, while other activists challenged lynching and discrimination outside mainstream white reform organizations.

Women's suffrage expanded formal citizenship, but discriminatory state practices continued to block many Black women and other women of color from voting.

:::warning
“Progressive” does not mean a person supported every form of equality. Evaluate a reformer issue by issue: someone could support labor laws and also defend racial segregation or immigration restrictions.
:::

## World War I

### From neutrality to intervention

The United States initially remained neutral when war began in Europe in 1914. Trade and loans increasingly connected it to the Allies. German submarine warfare threatened shipping and American lives, including in the sinking of the **Lusitania** in 1915.

Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare and the **Zimmermann Telegram**, proposing a German-Mexican alliance against the United States, helped push Wilson toward war in 1917. Wilson presented intervention as a defense of democracy and a chance to shape the peace.

### Mobilization and civil liberties

The federal government used the draft, coordinated production through agencies such as the **War Industries Board**, promoted food conservation, and sold war bonds. The **Committee on Public Information** mobilized support through propaganda.

The **Espionage Act** and **Sedition Act** restricted dissent. Socialists, labor activists, and critics of the war faced prosecution. In **Schenck v. United States**, the Supreme Court upheld a wartime conviction, illustrating the narrow protection given to some speech during the conflict.

Labor demand accelerated the **Great Migration** of Black southerners to northern and midwestern cities. Migrants sought jobs and escape from Jim Crow, but they encountered housing discrimination and racial violence. Women also entered additional jobs during mobilization.

:::key{name="War and the state"}
Mobilization increased the government's role in production, labor, information, and policing. The same war described as a defense of democracy also brought restrictions on dissent at home.
:::

### The peace settlement

Wilson's **Fourteen Points** proposed open diplomacy, freer trade, self-determination, and a **League of Nations**. The Treaty of Versailles reflected compromises with Allied leaders who sought security, territory, and reparations.

The Senate rejected U.S. membership in the League. Some senators opposed it outright; others, including **Henry Cabot Lodge**, wanted reservations protecting congressional authority over war. Wilson's refusal to accept major compromises helped defeat ratification.

The **First Red Scare** after the war combined fear of Bolshevism, labor unrest, and anarchist violence. The **Palmer Raids** targeted suspected radicals, often violating civil liberties. The racial violence of the **Red Summer of 1919** exposed the limits of wartime promises of democracy.

## The 1920s: prosperity and cultural conflict

Mass production, especially the automobile assembly line, lowered costs and supported consumer industries. Advertising and installment credit encouraged purchases of cars, radios, and household appliances. Republican administrations generally favored business, lower taxes, and limited regulation.

Prosperity was uneven. Farmers faced low prices and debt, many workers had limited purchasing power, and racial discrimination restricted opportunity. Rapid stock speculation did not mean the economy was equally healthy for everyone.

### Social and cultural change

The **Harlem Renaissance** celebrated Black art, literature, and intellectual life. **Langston Hughes**, **Zora Neale Hurston**, and other figures explored Black experience and identity. **Marcus Garvey** promoted Black pride, economic independence, and Pan-African identification.

The image of the **flapper** symbolized changing gender expectations, but most women did not experience complete social or economic freedom. Movies, radio, and sports produced a more national mass culture.

| Conflict | What was at stake |
| --- | --- |
| Scopes trial, 1925 | Evolution, public education, and religious authority |
| Prohibition | Moral reform, individual behavior, enforcement, and organized crime |
| Revived Ku Klux Klan | White Protestant supremacy directed against Black Americans, immigrants, Catholics, and Jews |
| Immigration quotas, 1921 and 1924 | Restriction of immigration, especially from southern and eastern Europe, alongside severe Asian exclusion |

:::note
The United States avoided some formal political commitments during the 1920s, but it remained involved through trade, loans, arms agreements, and intervention in Latin America. “Isolationism” can obscure this continued involvement.
:::

## The Great Depression

The **stock market crash of 1929** was a major shock, but the Depression had multiple causes. Unequal income distribution limited purchasing power; farmers and businesses faced overproduction; banks were vulnerable to failures; debt and speculation increased risk; and international financial problems spread the crisis.

Bank failures destroyed savings and restricted credit. Falling demand led firms to cut production and jobs, which reduced demand further. The **Smoot-Hawley Tariff** and retaliatory tariffs worsened the contraction of international trade.

President **Herbert Hoover** initially emphasized voluntary cooperation and local relief. He later supported measures such as the **Reconstruction Finance Corporation**, but his response remained too limited for the scale of unemployment and hardship. The removal of the **Bonus Army** from Washington in 1932 further damaged his reputation.

During the Depression, deportation drives and coerced departures sent many people of Mexican ancestry to Mexico, including U.S. citizens. These **Mexican repatriation** campaigns reflected efforts to reserve jobs and relief for people officials considered more deserving, with racial identity often taking precedence over citizenship.

The **Dust Bowl** combined drought with farming practices that left soil vulnerable to erosion. Displaced families moved in search of work, often facing hostility in destination states.

:::warning
The crash did not single-handedly cause the Depression, and Hoover did not literally do nothing. The stronger explanation identifies structural weaknesses and why his interventions failed to restore demand and confidence.
:::

## The New Deal

**Franklin D. Roosevelt** expanded federal action through programs often grouped as **relief, recovery, and reform**. These goals overlapped: one program could provide immediate jobs while also building long-term infrastructure.

| Program or law | Main function |
| --- | --- |
| FDIC | Insures bank deposits to reduce the risk of bank runs |
| CCC and WPA | Provide jobs through conservation and public projects |
| AAA | Seeks to raise agricultural prices by limiting production |
| TVA | Builds regional infrastructure and expands electricity access |
| Social Security Act | Establishes old-age benefits and other forms of social insurance and assistance |
| Wagner Act | Protects collective bargaining and establishes the National Labor Relations Board |
| SEC | Regulates securities markets |
| Fair Labor Standards Act | Establishes federal wage and hour standards and restricts child labor |

### Opposition and limitations

Conservatives argued that the New Deal expanded federal power too far. Critics such as **Huey Long** argued that it did too little to redistribute wealth. The Supreme Court struck down some early programs, and Roosevelt's proposed **court expansion plan** damaged his political standing.

The New Deal did not end racial or gender inequality. Agricultural and domestic workers were initially excluded from major protections, disproportionately affecting Black workers and women. Agricultural policy could displace tenant farmers, and local administration often reinforced discrimination.

The **New Deal coalition** brought together many urban workers, immigrants, white southerners, and an increasing share of Black voters. The federal government assumed greater responsibility for economic security, but capitalism and private ownership remained central.

:::key{name="The New Deal's significance"}
The New Deal changed expectations about federal responsibility for jobs, banking, retirement, and labor rights. It reduced suffering and reformed institutions, but full recovery came with the much larger mobilization of World War II.
:::

## World War II

### From neutrality to global war

During the 1930s, **Neutrality Acts** reflected fears of repeating the path into World War I. As Axis aggression expanded, Roosevelt moved toward aid to the Allies through measures such as **Lend-Lease**.

Japan's attack on **Pearl Harbor** on December 7, 1941, brought the United States into the war. Germany and Italy then declared war on the United States. The Allies pursued a **Europe-first** strategy while fighting Japan in the Pacific.

- **Midway** checked Japanese expansion and shifted the naval balance in the Pacific.
- **D-Day** opened a major western front in France in June 1944.
- **Island hopping** captured strategically important Pacific positions while bypassing others.
- The Soviet Union bore enormous losses fighting Germany on the eastern front; Allied victory depended on a coalition, not U.S. action alone.

### Mobilization and inequality at home

Federal spending, industrial conversion, rationing, and mass military service reorganized the economy. Women entered industrial work in greater numbers, though many were pushed out of those jobs after the war. The **Bracero Program** recruited Mexican workers under temporary labor agreements.

The **Double V campaign** called for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home. After **A. Philip Randolph** threatened a march on Washington, Roosevelt prohibited discrimination in defense employment and created the **Fair Employment Practice Committee**. Enforcement remained uneven, and the armed forces remained segregated during the war.

**Executive Order 9066** enabled the forced removal and incarceration of people of Japanese ancestry, most of them U.S. citizens. In **Korematsu v. United States**, the Supreme Court upheld the challenged exclusion order. There was no individualized proof that the people removed posed a security threat.

:::warning
Wartime opportunity and wartime repression coexisted. Industrial jobs expanded for some groups while racial discrimination, the Zoot Suit Riots, and Japanese American incarceration exposed major limits on equal citizenship.
:::

### Victory and the postwar order

Germany surrendered in May 1945. In August, the United States dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima and Nagasaki**, and the Soviet Union entered the war against Japan. Japan surrendered soon afterward. The relative importance of these pressures and the alternatives available to U.S. leaders remain subjects of historical debate.

The Holocaust and other wartime atrocities exposed the consequences of Nazi racial ideology and mass murder. U.S. immigration restrictions had limited refuge for many people fleeing persecution.

The war left the United States with enormous industrial and military power. The **United Nations** and new international financial institutions reflected efforts to prevent another global collapse, while tensions with the Soviet Union laid the groundwork for the Cold War.

## Connections and recap

| Historical connection | Evidence from this period |
| --- | --- |
| Expanding federal responsibility | Progressive regulation, wartime mobilization, and the New Deal |
| Democracy and exclusion | Colonial rule, speech restrictions, immigration quotas, and incarceration |
| Migration and social change | Great Migration, urban culture, and wartime employment |
| Global power | Overseas empire, world wars, and international institutions |

:::summary{title="Period 7"}
Industrial and international crises repeatedly expanded the federal government's role. Reform and war created new opportunities but also reinforced exclusions. By 1945, the United States had become a global power with lasting economic responsibilities at home and major military commitments abroad.
:::
