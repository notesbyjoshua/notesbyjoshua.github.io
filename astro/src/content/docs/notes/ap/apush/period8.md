## The origins of the Cold War

The **Cold War** was a prolonged rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union involving ideology, military power, economic systems, and influence over other countries. Their wartime alliance broke down as they disagreed over Eastern Europe, Germany, and the postwar balance of power.

The Soviet Union sought security through control over neighboring states. U.S. leaders viewed Soviet expansion as a threat to self-determination and an open international economy. Each side interpreted the other's actions as evidence of aggressive intentions.

**Containment**, associated with diplomat **George Kennan**, aimed to prevent the spread of Soviet power rather than directly overthrow the Soviet government.

| Policy or event | Purpose and significance |
| --- | --- |
| Truman Doctrine, 1947 | Supports Greece and Turkey against perceived communist pressure |
| Marshall Plan | Funds European recovery, helping stabilize economies and reduce communist appeal |
| Berlin Airlift, 1948–1949 | Supplies West Berlin during a Soviet blockade |
| NATO, 1949 | Creates a lasting collective-defense alliance |
| NSC-68, 1950 | Calls for a large military buildup to confront a global Soviet threat |

The communist victory in China and the Soviet atomic test in 1949 heightened U.S. fears. Nuclear weapons made direct superpower war increasingly dangerous, encouraging competition through alliances, arms races, and conflicts elsewhere.

:::key{name="Containment"}
Containment could use economic aid, diplomacy, military alliances, covert intervention, or war. Do not reduce it to a single program or assume every president used the same methods.
:::

## Cold War conflicts and intervention

### Korea and the nuclear arms race

North Korea invaded South Korea in 1950. A U.S.-led United Nations force intervened. After UN troops advanced north, China entered the war. The fighting ended in an armistice in 1953 near the original dividing line, leaving Korea divided.

President Truman dismissed **Douglas MacArthur** after disagreements over expanding the war. The episode reinforced civilian control of the military and the difficulty of fighting a limited war in a nuclear age.

Under **Dwight Eisenhower**, nuclear deterrence and the threat of **massive retaliation** became central to strategy. The launch of **Sputnik** in 1957 intensified the space race and encouraged federal investment in science and education. Eisenhower later warned about the influence of the **military-industrial complex**.

### Intervention and decolonization

As colonies gained independence, U.S. leaders often viewed local struggles through an anticommunist lens. Covert operations in **Iran in 1953** and **Guatemala in 1954** helped overthrow governments regarded as threats to U.S. interests. Such interventions could undermine democratic institutions while being justified as defenses of freedom.

In Cuba, the failed **Bay of Pigs invasion** in 1961 damaged Kennedy's administration. The **Cuban Missile Crisis** in 1962 ended through negotiation: Soviet missiles left Cuba, the United States pledged not to invade, and U.S. missiles were later removed from Turkey under a private agreement.

:::warning
Local conflicts were not simply contests controlled by Washington and Moscow. Nationalism, colonial rule, land reform, and domestic political struggles shaped events in countries where the superpowers intervened.
:::

## Anticommunism at home

The Cold War produced a **Second Red Scare**. Loyalty programs, investigations, and prosecutions sought to expose communist influence. Soviet espionage was real, but suspicion spread far beyond proven spying.

The **House Un-American Activities Committee** investigated alleged subversion, including in Hollywood. Blacklists cost people employment without requiring criminal convictions. Senator **Joseph McCarthy** gained influence through sweeping accusations, often unsupported by evidence.

Government campaigns also targeted gay and lesbian employees during the **Lavender Scare**, treating sexuality as a supposed security risk. Political dissent and private identity could both become grounds for exclusion.

:::key{name="Security and liberty"}
Separate evidence of espionage from the wider use of accusation and guilt by association. A real security threat does not establish that every investigation, dismissal, or restriction was justified.
:::

## Postwar prosperity and its limits

Economic growth rested on wartime industrial strength, consumer demand, federal spending, technological development, and a strong international position. The **GI Bill** supported education and homeownership for veterans, but discrimination in lending, admissions, and local administration limited access for many Black veterans.

### Suburbs, the Sun Belt, and consumer culture

Mass-produced housing, automobiles, and the **Interstate Highway System** encouraged suburban growth. The **baby boom** increased demand for schools, homes, and consumer goods. Television helped spread national advertising and cultural expectations.

**Redlining**, restrictive covenants, and discriminatory lending reinforced residential segregation. As people and investment moved outward, many central cities lost part of their tax base. Suburban prosperity therefore depended partly on public policy and unequal access to credit.

The **Sun Belt** grew through defense spending, new industries, air conditioning, and migration. This shift increased the economic and political influence of the South and West.

### Work, gender, and dissent

Popular culture often celebrated the male breadwinner and female homemaker, but many women continued paid work. Labor unions secured gains for many industrial workers, while the **Taft-Hartley Act** limited union power.

Beat writers and later youth movements criticized conformity, materialism, and restrictive social expectations. Postwar affluence did not remove poverty, racial inequality, or dissatisfaction with conventional family and work roles.

:::note
The 1950s were neither universally prosperous nor uniformly conformist. Growth was substantial, but access depended on race, gender, occupation, and location, and organized challenges were already developing.
:::

## The Black freedom struggle

The civil rights movement grew from decades of local organizing, legal challenges, labor activism, churches, and community institutions. National leaders mattered, but sustained change depended on many people whose names received less publicity.

### From desegregation to federal legislation

| Event or organization | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Brown v. Board of Education, 1954 | Rejects racial segregation in public schools as unconstitutional |
| Montgomery bus boycott, 1955–1956 | Uses sustained collective action against segregated transportation |
| Little Rock, 1957 | Federal troops enforce school desegregation against state resistance |
| Sit-ins and Freedom Rides | Challenge segregation through direct action |
| SCLC and SNCC | Organize campaigns, voter registration, and community action |
| Birmingham campaign and March on Washington, 1963 | Build pressure for federal civil rights action |
| Civil Rights Act, 1964 | Prohibits major forms of segregation and employment discrimination |
| Voting Rights Act, 1965 | Strengthens federal action against racial barriers to voting |
| Fair Housing Act, 1968 | Prohibits major forms of housing discrimination |

**Martin Luther King Jr.** promoted nonviolent direct action to expose injustice and force negotiation. Organizers such as **Ella Baker**, **Fannie Lou Hamer**, and local student activists built participation and challenged dependence on a single charismatic leader.

### Beyond formal segregation

Legal victories did not eliminate housing discrimination, unequal schools, unemployment, or police violence. **Malcolm X** emphasized Black dignity and self-determination, with his views changing over time. **Black Power** activists stressed community control, political independence, and pride. The **Black Panther Party** combined armed self-defense with community programs.

Urban uprisings reflected frustrations that legislative reform had not resolved. Debates over tactics did not erase shared opposition to racial oppression.

:::warning
The Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act were major turning points, not the end of racism. Distinguish the removal of explicit legal barriers from the elimination of economic and social inequality.
:::

## Liberal reform and expanding rights movements

### The Great Society

President **Lyndon Johnson's Great Society** expanded federal efforts in health care, education, poverty reduction, and civil rights.

- **Medicare** provided health insurance mainly for older Americans; **Medicaid** supported eligible people with low incomes through a federal-state system.
- **Head Start** and education funding sought to reduce unequal opportunity.
- The **War on Poverty** supported community programs and job training.
- The **Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965** ended the national-origins quota system, helping reshape later immigration from Asia and Latin America.

The Warren Court also expanded protections for defendants and addressed representation. **Gideon v. Wainwright** protected access to counsel in state felony cases, and **Miranda v. Arizona** required warnings before custodial interrogation.

### Youth activism and the counterculture

The **New Left**, including **Students for a Democratic Society**, criticized concentrated power and called for participatory democracy. Campus organizers challenged university restrictions, racial injustice, and the Vietnam War. The **counterculture** questioned conventional expectations about consumption, sexuality, dress, and authority through music, communal living, and other experiments. Political activism and cultural rebellion overlapped, but they were not the same movement.

### Other movements

| Movement | Main concerns and examples |
| --- | --- |
| Women's movement | Employment, education, reproductive autonomy, and unequal domestic expectations; NOW and Title IX |
| Chicano movement | Farm labor, educational equality, political representation, and cultural pride; César Chávez and Dolores Huerta |
| Native activism | Sovereignty, treaty rights, and control of community life; AIM and the occupation of Alcatraz |
| Gay liberation | Resistance to criminalization and discrimination; organizing accelerated after Stonewall in 1969 |
| Environmental movement | Pollution, pesticides, and conservation; Rachel Carson, Earth Day, and the EPA |

**Roe v. Wade** in 1973 recognized constitutional protection for abortion and intensified political conflict. The **Equal Rights Amendment** passed Congress but failed to gain enough state ratifications by its deadline. Its opponents, including **Phyllis Schlafly**, helped mobilize conservative women.

:::key{name="A shared opening, different goals"}
Civil rights victories provided strategies and language for other movements, but each addressed distinct conditions. Native demands for sovereignty, for example, went beyond inclusion as equal individuals within existing institutions.
:::

## Vietnam and the crisis of confidence

U.S. involvement in Vietnam grew from containment, the **domino theory**, and support for an anticommunist South Vietnamese government after French colonial rule ended. Kennedy expanded advisers; Johnson escalated after the **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution** in 1964.

Large troop deployments and bombing failed to produce the quick victory officials promised. The **Tet Offensive** in 1968 was a military setback for communist forces but a major political turning point because it undermined claims that U.S. victory was near.

The draft, casualties, television reporting, and revelations such as the **My Lai massacre** expanded opposition. The **Pentagon Papers** exposed gaps between official statements and internal assessments. Protests varied from student activism to veterans' opposition; many Americans also continued to support the war.

Nixon pursued **Vietnamization**, shifting combat responsibilities to South Vietnamese forces while withdrawing U.S. troops. He also expanded operations into Cambodia. The **Paris Peace Accords** in 1973 ended direct U.S. combat involvement, but fighting continued until North Vietnam's victory in 1975.

:::warning
The Vietnam War did not end in 1973 for the Vietnamese. That date marks the U.S. agreement and withdrawal; the war continued until 1975.
:::

## The 1970s: détente, scandal, and economic trouble

### Foreign policy

Nixon pursued **détente**, easing tensions through diplomacy with the Soviet Union and an opening to China. **SALT I** limited some nuclear weapons systems, but competition and intervention continued.

President **Jimmy Carter** emphasized human rights and helped negotiate the **Camp David Accords** between Egypt and Israel. The **Iran hostage crisis** and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979 damaged confidence in his foreign policy and weakened détente.

### Watergate and distrust

The **Watergate scandal** involved a break-in at Democratic headquarters and efforts to cover up wrongdoing. Investigations revealed broader abuses of executive power. Nixon resigned in 1974 as impeachment became likely. The scandal, together with Vietnam, deepened distrust of government.

The **War Powers Resolution** of 1973 attempted to limit unilateral presidential military commitments. It reflected congressional efforts to reassert authority after Vietnam.

### Stagflation and conservatism

**Stagflation** combined high inflation with weak growth and unemployment. Oil shocks, productivity problems, international competition, and other pressures challenged postwar economic assumptions. Deindustrialization damaged many older manufacturing communities.

Conservative activists criticized taxes, regulation, welfare programs, and changes in social norms. The **Southern Strategy**, suburban organizing, business mobilization, and the rise of the **religious right** helped expand the Republican coalition. Conservative growth was gradual and involved more than a reaction to one president or one policy.

| --- | --- |

:::summary{title="Period 8"}
The Cold War made global leadership a lasting U.S. commitment. Economic growth and federal programs expanded opportunity, while social movements challenged the exclusions built into postwar society. War, scandal, and economic instability weakened confidence in liberal government and helped conservatives gain influence by 1980.
:::
