1. A period-3 element X has successive ionization energies of approximately $$740,1450,7730,10500\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. For its usual monatomic ion, which electron count and radius comparison with $$\mathrm{Na^+}$$ are consistent with these data?

   (A) 10 electrons; smaller than sodium ion<br>
   (B) 10 electrons; larger than sodium ion<br>
   (C) 12 electrons; smaller than sodium ion<br>
   (D) 8 electrons; larger than sodium ion

:::solution
The large jump after the second removal identifies two valence electrons, so X is magnesium and its usual ion is magnesium(II). It has 10 electrons, just like sodium ion, but 12 protons rather than 11. With the same occupied shells, the stronger nuclear attraction makes the magnesium ion smaller.

$$
\boxed{\text{A}}.
$$

::::problem
2. A hypothetical element has isotopes of masses $$50.0$$ and $$52.0\ \mathrm{amu}$$ and an average atomic mass of $$50.6\ \mathrm{amu}$$. If atoms pair randomly to form diatomic molecules, what fraction contains one atom of each isotope?

   (A) $$0.21$$<br>
   (B) $$0.30$$<br>
   (C) $$0.42$$<br>
   (D) $$0.49$$

:::solution
The heavy-isotope fraction satisfies $$50(1-f)+52f=50.6$$, giving $$f=0.30$$. Mixed molecules arise in two ways, light-heavy and heavy-light, so their fraction is $$2(0.70)(0.30)=0.42$$. Using just one product misses half the mixed pairs.

$$
\boxed{\text{C}}.
$$
:::

::::problem
3. A surface has threshold wavelength $$600\ \mathrm{nm}$$. Equal-energy pulses of $$300\ \mathrm{nm}$$ and $$400\ \mathrm{nm}$$ light strike it. In the single-photon model, what are $$N_{400}/N_{300}$$ and $$K_{max,300}/K_{max,400}$$?

   (A) $$3/4$$ and $$4/3$$<br>
   (B) $$4/3$$ and $$2$$<br>
   (C) $$4/3$$ and $$4/3$$<br>
   (D) $$3/4$$ and $$2$$

:::solution
Equal pulse energy gives photon count proportional to wavelength, hence $$N_{400}/N_{300}=4/3$$. The threshold work function is $$hc/(600\ \mathrm{nm})$$. Subtracting this from each photon energy gives a kinetic-energy ratio $$(1/300-1/600)/(1/400-1/600)=2$$. Photon-energy ratios alone do not give the electron kinetic-energy ratio.

$$
\boxed{\text{B}}.
$$
:::

4. A compound contains $$40.0\%$$ C, $$6.7\%$$ H, and the remainder O. Its molar mass is $$90.0\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$. Use atomic masses 12.0, 1.00, and 16.0. Which molecular formula is consistent with both measurements?

   (A) $$\mathrm{CH_2O}$$<br>
   (B) $$\mathrm{C_3H_3O_3}$$<br>
   (C) $$\mathrm{C_2H_4O_2}$$<br>
   (D) $$\mathrm{C_3H_6O_3}$$

:::solution
Oxygen is 53.3% by mass. A 100 g sample gives approximately 3.33 mol C, 6.7 mol H, and 3.33 mol O, a 1:2:1 ratio. The empirical mass is 30.0 g/mol, so the molecular formula is three times the empirical formula. Neither the mass percentages nor the molar mass can be ignored.

\boxed{\text{D}}.
$$

   (B) $$0.0250\ M$$<br>

   (D) $$0.100\ M$$

:::solution
The hydrate supplies $$2.50/250.=0.0100\ \mathrm{mol}$$ copper ions, giving a 0.100 M stock. The transferred portion contains $$0.100(0.0150)=0.00150\ \mathrm{mol}$$ copper ions. Dividing by 0.0600 L gives 0.0250 M. Waters of hydration contribute to the solid's molar mass but do not multiply the copper amount.

\boxed{\text{B}}.

6. Neutral X and Y have valence configurations $$3s^2$$ and $$3s^23p^1$$, respectively. Which paired prediction is correct?

   (A) Y has both the higher first ionization energy and larger usual cation<br>
   (B) X has the smaller neutral atom and smaller usual cation<br>
   (C) X has the higher first ionization energy, but Y has the smaller usual cation<br>
   (D) Y has the higher first ionization energy, but X has the smaller usual cation

:::solution
X is Mg and Y is Al. The first electron removed from Al occupies the higher-energy, less penetrating 3p subshell, so its first ionization energy is lower than Mg's. The usual ions, magnesium(II) and aluminum(III), both have 10 electrons; aluminum's greater nuclear charge makes its ion smaller. Different comparisons require different electron configurations.

::::frq{id=chem-atomicstrucprop-challenge-1}
7. A hypothetical element X forms singly charged ions with mass-spectrum peaks at $$m/z=78.0$$ and $$80.0$$, with relative intensities 100 and 25. Assume equal detector response. A molecular compound of X and fluorine contains 57.9% X by mass and has molar mass $$135.4\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$. Use $$M_F=19.0\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$ and $$N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$$.

   $$(A)$$ Determine the isotope abundances and X's average atomic mass.

   $$(B)$$ Determine the empirical and molecular formulas of the compound from the composition data.

   $$(C)$$ Find the number of molecules containing the heavier X isotope in a 0.6770 g sample. Determine the masses of its two isotope-specific molecular ions, treating fluorine as mass 19.0.

   $$(D)$$ Predict the relative molecular-ion peak intensities and explain how doubly charged ions would change their positions.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ Normalize by the total intensity, 125. The abundances are $$100/125=80.0\%$$ and $$25/125=20.0\%$$. The average is $$0.800(78.0)+0.200(80.0)=78.4\ \mathrm{amu}$$. A peak normalized to 100 is not a statement that every atom has that mass.

$$(B)$$ For 100 g, $$57.9/78.4=0.7385\ \mathrm{mol}$$ X and $$42.1/19.0=2.216\ \mathrm{mol}$$ F give approximately a 1:3 ratio. The empirical formula is $$\mathrm{XF_3}$$, with mass $$78.4+3(19.0)=135.4\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$. This matches the measured molar mass, so the multiplier is one and the molecular formula is also $$\mathrm{XF_3}$$. The isotope-weighted average, rather than either individual isotope mass, is used for the bulk sample.

$$(C)$$ Total compound amount is $$0.6770/135.4=0.005000\ \mathrm{mol}$$. One X atom occurs in each molecule, so 20.0% of molecules contain the heavier isotope: $$N=0.200(0.005000)(6.022\times10^{23})=6.02\times10^{20}$$ molecules. Individual molecular ions have masses $$78.0+57.0=135.0$$ and $$80.0+57.0=137.0\ \mathrm{amu}$$, not the average formula mass.

$$(D)$$ With one X atom per molecule, the isotope fractions remain 80.0% and 20.0%, so equal-response peak intensities retain a 4:1 ratio. Singly charged peaks occur at 135.0 and 137.0; doubly charged versions would occur at $$m/z=67.5$$ and $$68.5$$. Charge changes peak position without changing isotope mass. Comparing intensities across charge states would require knowing their relative formation and detector responses.
:::
::::

::::frq{id=chem-atomicstrucprop-challenge-2}
8. A period-3 element Y has subshell electron counts $$2,2,6,2,1$$ in order of decreasing binding energy. A metal surface has work function $$\Phi=3.00\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$$. Use $$h=6.63\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$$ and $$c=3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$$.

   $$(A)$$ Identify Y and its usual cation's electron configuration. Predict whether that cation or magnesium(II) is smaller, and justify.

   $$(B)$$ Explain why Y can have a lower first ionization energy than magnesium despite its larger nuclear charge, then predict where Y's largest early successive-ionization jump occurs.

   $$(C)$$ Calculate the maximum electron kinetic energy when the surface is illuminated at $$400.\ \mathrm{nm}$$ and determine the longest wavelength that can eject electrons.

   $$(D)$$ Equal-energy pulses of 400 nm and 800 nm light strike the surface. Compare photon counts and decide which pulse ejects electrons in the single-photon model.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ The counts give $$1s^22s^22p^63s^23p^1$$, totaling 13 electrons: aluminum. Removing the three valence electrons gives $$\mathrm{Al^{3+}}:[\mathrm{Ne}]$$. Both aluminum(III) and magnesium(II) have 10 electrons, but aluminum has 13 protons instead of 12. Its greater nuclear attraction contracts the same occupied electron shells more strongly, so aluminum(III) is smaller.

$$(B)$$ Aluminum's first removed electron is in 3p, which is higher in energy and less penetrating than magnesium's 3s electron. That subshell difference makes removal easier despite the larger proton count. After aluminum loses three valence electrons, removing a fourth electron disrupts the neon-like core. The large jump is therefore between the third and fourth ionization energies, not between the first and second.

$$(C)$$ Photon energy is $$hc/\lambda=(6.63\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^8)/(4.00\times10^{-7})=4.97\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$$. Subtracting the work function gives $$K_{max}=1.97\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$$. At threshold, $$hc/\lambda_0=\Phi$$, so $$\lambda_0=6.63\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}=663\ \mathrm{nm}$$. Longer wavelengths carry too little energy per photon.

$$(D)$$ At fixed pulse energy, photon count is proportional to wavelength, so the 800 nm pulse contains twice as many photons. Its photon energy is only $$2.49\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$$, below the work function. It ejects no electrons in the stated model, while 400 nm photons can eject electrons. Greater photon count cannot compensate for insufficient energy per individual photon.
:::

1. For $$\mathrm{CH_3-C\equiv N}$$, which combination of total sigma bonds, total pi bonds, and carbon hybridizations is correct in the localized bonding model?

   (A) 5 sigma, 2 pi; methyl carbon $$sp^3$$ and nitrile carbon $$sp$$<br>
   (B) 3 sigma, 3 pi; both carbons $$sp^2$$<br>
   (C) 5 sigma, 1 pi; both carbons $$sp^3$$<br>
   (D) 6 sigma, 2 pi; methyl carbon $$sp^3$$ and nitrile carbon $$sp^2$$

:::solution
Three C-H bonds contribute three sigma bonds, the C-C connection contributes one, and C-N contributes one sigma plus two pi bonds. The methyl carbon has four domains and is sp3; the nitrile carbon has two domains and is sp. A triple bond counts as one domain but contains three bonding interactions.

$$
\boxed{\text{A}}.
$$

::::problem
2. An octet-obeying nitrate contributor has one N=O bond and two N-O bonds. Which statement correctly connects formal charge with measured bond lengths?

   (A) Nitrogen is neutral and one bond remains permanently shorter<br>
   (B) Nitrogen is $$+1$$, each single-bonded oxygen is $$-1$$, and all three real N-O bonds are equivalent<br>
   (C) Nitrogen is $$-1$$ and resonance requires the atoms to exchange positions<br>
   (D) Every oxygen is $$-1$$ and the real ion has one localized double bond

:::solution
Nitrogen is assigned four bonding electrons, so its formal charge is $$5-4=+1$$. Each single-bonded oxygen is -1 and the double-bonded oxygen is neutral, totaling -1. Three equivalent contributors place the double bond on different oxygens, describing delocalized bonding rather than permanently different bond lengths.

$$
\boxed{\text{B}}.
$$

::::problem
3. Pair X consists of $$+1$$ and $$-1$$ ions separated by r. Pair Y consists of $$+2$$ and $$-1$$ ions separated by $$1.5r$$. What are the ratios $$\lvert U_Y\rvert/\lvert U_X\rvert$$ and $$F_Y/F_X$$?

   (A) $$4/3$$ and $$4/3$$<br>
   (B) $$8/9$$ and $$4/3$$<br>
   (C) $$4/3$$ and $$8/9$$<br>
   (D) $$2$$ and $$2/3$$

:::solution
Potential energy scales as charge product divided by distance, giving $$2/1.5=4/3$$. Force magnitude instead scales inversely with distance squared, giving $$2/(1.5)^2=8/9$$. A more negative pair energy can coexist with a smaller force at the specified separation; energy and force are not interchangeable.

\boxed{\text{C}}.
$$

4. For a hypothetical MX salt, atomizing the elements costs $$180\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$, ionizing M costs $$500$$, and adding an electron to X releases $$320$$. The solid's formation enthalpy is $$-410\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. What is the energy to separate the crystal into gaseous ions?

   (A) $$90\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$<br>
   (B) $$410\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$<br>
   (C) $$-770\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$<br>
   (D) $$770\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$

:::solution
Gas-ion preparation costs $$180+500-320=360\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. If U is the positive lattice-separation energy, Hess's law gives $$-410=360-U$$, so $$U=770\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. Lattice formation has the opposite sign; the requested process is separation.

\boxed{\text{D}}.

5. In $$\mathrm{XeF_4}$$, a Lewis structure gives xenon four bonds and two lone pairs. Which arrangement minimizes lone-pair repulsion and what molecular polarity follows?

   (A) Adjacent lone pairs; polar tetrahedral molecule<br>
   (B) Opposite lone pairs; nonpolar square-planar molecule<br>
   (C) Opposite lone pairs; polar seesaw molecule<br>
   (D) Adjacent lone pairs; nonpolar trigonal-planar molecule

:::solution
Six domains give an octahedral electron arrangement. Placing the two lone pairs opposite each other leaves four fluorines in a square plane. Equal Xe-F bond dipoles cancel by symmetry. Counting bonded atoms alone would miss both the electron-domain geometry and the lone-pair placement.

\boxed{\text{B}}.

6. Two isomers are $$\mathrm{CH_3CH_2OH}$$ and $$\mathrm{CH_3OCH_3}$$. Which explanation correctly predicts the higher boiling point without confusing intermolecular forces with covalent bonding?

   (A) The ether, because its two C-O bonds must break during boiling<br>
   (B) Ethanol, because it has more electrons<br>

:::solution
Both isomers have the same formula and electron count, and both experience dispersion forces. Ethanol has an O-H donor as well as oxygen lone pairs, enabling self-hydrogen-bonding; ether has no suitable donor. Boiling separates molecules rather than breaking their C-O bonds.

#### FRQ

::::frq{id=chem-compounds-challenge-1}
7. Consider formic acid, $$\mathrm{H-C(=O)-O-H}$$, and its conjugate base, formate, $$\mathrm{HCO_2^-}$$. Use the localized Lewis and hybridization models for individual contributors.

   $$(A)$$ Draw a complete Lewis structure of formic acid, including lone pairs. Count all sigma and pi bonds and assign the carbon hybridization.

   $$(B)$$ Write the two equivalent formate resonance contributors and assign formal charges in each. Predict the two C-O bond lengths relative to the single and double C-O bonds of the acid.

   $$(C)$$ The O-C-O angle is close to 120 degrees. Explain why counting four bond lines around carbon would give the wrong geometry.

   $$(D)$$ Compare the interactions of each species with water. Explain why the absence of an O-H bond in formate does not imply weak attraction to water.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ The connectivity is $$\mathrm{H-C(=O)-O-H}$$, with two lone pairs on each oxygen. It has four sigma bonds: C-H, two C-O connections, and O-H. The carbonyl contributes one pi bond. Carbon has three electron domains and is sp2, with its unhybridized p orbital participating in the pi bond. Both oxygens and carbon have octets, and each atom has zero formal charge.

$$(B)$$ The contributors are $$\mathrm{H-C(=O)-O^-}$$ and $$\mathrm{H-C(-O^-)=O}$$. In each, the single-bonded oxygen has three lone pairs and formal charge -1; the double-bonded oxygen has two lone pairs and charge 0. Carbon and hydrogen are neutral. Delocalization makes the formate C-O bonds equivalent, with lengths between typical C-O single and double bonds. A drawing with one localized double bond is not the complete description of the ion.

$$(C)$$ A double bond contains sigma and pi interactions along one bonded direction, so it counts as one electron domain. Carbon has three domains: one toward hydrogen and one toward each oxygen. Their approximately trigonal-planar arrangement gives angles near 120 degrees. Four drawn bond lines do not indicate four independent directions or tetrahedral geometry.

$$(D)$$ Formic acid can donate a hydrogen bond from O-H and accept hydrogen bonds at oxygen. Formate lacks an O-H donor, but its negatively charged, delocalized oxygen region interacts strongly with water's partial positive hydrogen ends through ion-dipole interactions and can accept hydrogen bonds. Losing a donor does not remove the ion's charge or its acceptor sites. Comparing the two species requires considering charge as well as molecular functional groups.
:::
::::

::::frq{id=chem-compounds-challenge-2}
8. A hypothetical ionic solid $$\mathrm{MX_2}$$ contains $$\mathrm{M^{2+}}$$ and $$\mathrm{X^-}$$. Per mole of solid, metal sublimation costs 150 kJ, the first and second ionizations cost 600 and 1200 kJ, dissociating one mole of $$\mathrm{X_2}$$ costs 200 kJ, and adding an electron to each gaseous X atom releases 350 kJ. Formation of the solid from its standard-state elements has $$\Delta H_f^\circ=-700\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$.

   $$(A)$$ Write a Born-Haber sum and calculate the positive lattice-separation energy U.

   $$(B)$$ An otherwise comparable solid has the same charges and lattice arrangement but ion separation 10.0% larger. Use the inverse-distance model to estimate its lattice-separation energy. State a limitation of the estimate.

   $$(C)$$ If hydration of one mole of gaseous ion sets releases 2100 kJ, estimate the dissolution enthalpy of the original salt. Can the sign alone establish whether it dissolves?

   $$(D)$$ Predict electrical conduction in the solid, molten salt, and aqueous solution if the salt dissolves. Identify the moving particles rather than just saying there are charges.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ Preparing gaseous ions costs $$150+600+1200+200-2(350)=1450\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. Two electron-gain events are needed because the formula contains two anions. Formation of the lattice contributes -U, so $$-700=1450-U$$ and $$U=2150\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. The dissociation term is for one whole mole of diatomic X2, supplying two moles of atoms.

$$(B)$$ With all other model factors fixed, $$U'\approx U/1.10=1955\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$, about $$1.95\times10^3\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. Greater separation weakens attraction. Actual lattice energies also depend on repulsion, polarizability, and crystal structure, so simply scaling distance is an estimate rather than a complete calculation for a different real compound.

$$(C)$$ Lattice separation costs 2150 kJ/mol and hydration releases 2100 kJ/mol, giving $$\Delta H_{soln}=+50\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}$$. Dissolution is endothermic in this model. Its equilibrium extent also depends on entropy and free energy, so positive dissolution enthalpy alone does not prove insolubility.

$$(D)$$ In the ideal solid, ions are fixed in lattice positions and cannot sustain bulk charge transport. In the melt, mobile cations and anions carry current in opposite directions. Dissolved ions can also migrate through water and carry current. The ionic substance does not become a metal on melting; the main carriers remain ions rather than delocalized electrons.
:::

3. An ideal liquid mixture has pure-component vapor pressures $$P_A^*=100$$ and $$P_B^*=40\ \mathrm{torr}$$. Its equilibrium vapor is 80.0% A by mole. What is the liquid mole fraction of A?

   (C) $$0.500$$<br>

4. A $$0.100\ m$$ solution of a 1:1 electrolyte has freezing-point depression $$0.335^\circ\mathrm{C}$$ in water, with $$K_f=1.86^\circ\mathrm{C}/m$$. If undissociated formula units and separate ions are the only solute species, what fraction is dissociated?

   (C) $$0.50$$<br>
   (D) $$0.80$$

:::solution
The effective particle factor is $$i=0.335/(1.86\times0.100)=1.80$$. Each dissociated formula unit changes one particle into two, so $$i=1+\alpha$$. Therefore $$\alpha=0.80$$. The van't Hoff factor is a particle-count multiplier, not itself the fraction dissociated.

::::frq{id=chem-submix-challenge-1}
7. At a fixed wavelength, standards measured in a 1.00 cm cuvette follow $$A=25.0c+0.030$$, with c in M. A $$4.00\ \mathrm{mL}$$ unknown stock is diluted to $$100.0\ \mathrm{mL}$$. Its absorbance in a 2.00 cm cuvette is 0.430. The 0.030 instrument offset is independent of cuvette length.

   $$(A)$$ Calculate the diluted and stock concentrations, showing how path length and the offset enter separately.

   $$(B)$$ The stock contains a nonelectrolyte of molar mass $$150.\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$. Find the mass of solute in $$250.0\ \mathrm{mL}$$ of stock.

   $$(C)$$ Predict the measured absorbance if equal volumes of the diluted unknown and pure solvent are mixed, then measured in the same 2.00 cm cuvette.

   $$(D)$$ A fingerprint decreases transmitted light for the unknown but not the standards. Predict the stock-concentration error. Would a correct dilution calculation remove it?

:::solution
$$(A)$$ Corrected absorbance is $$0.430-0.030=0.400$$. Doubling the path doubles the solute response coefficient from 25.0 to 50.0, so $$c_{dil}=0.400/50.0=0.00800\ M$$. The dilution factor is $$100.0/4.00=25.0$$, giving $$c_{stock}=0.200\ M$$. Multiplying the offset by two would incorrectly treat an instrument bias as absorption by the solute.

$$(B)$$ The stock amount is $$n=(0.200)(0.2500)=0.0500\ \mathrm{mol}$$. Multiplying by molar mass gives $$m=0.0500(150.)=7.50\ \mathrm{g}$$. This uses the original stock concentration, not the 25-times-smaller concentration in the measured flask.

$$(C)$$ Equal-volume mixing halves solute concentration to 0.00400 M. The solute contribution becomes $$50.0(0.00400)=0.200$$, while the offset remains 0.030. The measured absorbance is therefore 0.230, not half of 0.430. Only the concentration-dependent part scales with dilution.

$$(D)$$ Lower transmitted intensity produces a larger measured absorbance because $$A=-\log(I/I_0)$$. The calibration then attributes the extra absorbance to solute, overestimating diluted concentration. Multiplying by the correct dilution factor carries the same relative error into the stock result. Correct volume arithmetic cannot remove an optical interference.
:::

   (D) $$\Delta S_{univ}=-10.0\ \mathrm{J/K}$$ and $$\Delta G=+3.00\ \mathrm{kJ}$$

:::solution
The surroundings gain $$9000/300=30.0\ \mathrm{J/K}$$, making the universe change $$30.0-20.0=10.0\ \mathrm{J/K}$$. Independently, $$\Delta G=-9.00-300(-0.0200)=-3.00\ \mathrm{kJ}$$. The system's entropy can decrease while the total entropy increases.

$$

5. Reduction potentials are $$E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{3+}/M^{2+}})=0.600\ V$$ and $$E^\circ(\mathrm{M^{2+}/M})=-0.300\ V$$. What is $$E^\circ$$ for $$\mathrm{M^{3+}+3e^-\rightarrow M}$$?

   (B) $$0.150\ V$$<br>

7. A galvanic cell follows $$\mathrm{Zn(s)+Cu^{2+}(aq)\rightarrow Zn^{2+}(aq)+Cu(s)}$$ with $$E^\circ=1.10\ V$$ at $$25^\circ\mathrm{C}$$. Initially each compartment has 1.00 L solution: $$[Zn^{2+}]=0.100\ M$$ and $$[Cu^{2+}]=0.500\ M$$. Use $$F=96485\ \mathrm{C/mol\ e^-}$$, a base-ten Nernst coefficient of 0.0592 V, and copper molar mass $$63.55\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$. Ignore volume change and competing reactions.

   $$(A)$$ Write both half-reactions, identify anode and cathode, and calculate the initial cell potential.

   $$(B)$$ After 0.100 mol copper has deposited, calculate both ion concentrations and the new cell potential.

   $$(C)$$ Find the charge passed and copper mass deposited during part B. Explain whether doubling electrode mass alone doubles voltage.

   $$(D)$$ Calculate the equilibrium constant and predict which salt-bridge ions move toward the zinc compartment during discharge.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ Zinc oxidation is $$\mathrm{Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-}$$ at the anode. Copper reduction is $$\mathrm{Cu^{2+}+2e^-\rightarrow Cu}$$ at the cathode. Initially $$Q=0.100/0.500=0.200$$, so $$E=1.10-(0.0592/2)\log0.200=1.121\ V$$. The solids are excluded from Q, and electrons flow externally from zinc to copper.

$$(B)$$ Each mole of deposited copper consumes one mole copper ions and produces one mole zinc ions. The 1.00 L compartments therefore become 0.400 M copper(II) and 0.200 M zinc(II). Now $$Q=0.200/0.400=0.500$$ and $$E=1.10-(0.0592/2)\log0.500=1.109\ V$$. Discharge raises Q and lowers the forward voltage.

$$(C)$$ Depositing 0.100 mol Cu requires 0.200 mol electrons, giving $$q=0.200F=1.93\times10^4\ C$$. Copper mass is $$0.100(63.55)=6.36\ \mathrm{g}$$. Adding more of a pure solid electrode does not change its activity or Q, so voltage does not double. More electrode material can increase available reaction capacity without increasing energy per unit charge.

$$(D)$$ At equilibrium, $$0=E^\circ-(0.0592/2)\log K$$, giving $$\log K=2(1.10)/0.0592=37.16$$ and $$K\approx1.45\times10^{37}$$. Zinc oxidation adds positive ions to its compartment, so salt-bridge anions migrate toward it to maintain charge balance. Electrons travel through the wire, not through the salt bridge.

8. A copper-plating cell runs at 2.50 A. Only 75.0% of the charge reduces copper(II) to copper; all remaining cathodic charge produces hydrogen gas from acid. The target copper deposit is 3.00 g. Use $$M_{Cu}=63.55\ \mathrm{g/mol}$$, $$F=96485\ \mathrm{C/mol\ e^-}$$, and $$R=0.0821\ \mathrm{L\,atm/(mol\,K)}$$.

   $$(A)$$ Calculate the useful deposition charge, total charge, and operating time.

   $$(B)$$ Calculate the moles and dry volume of hydrogen produced at 298 K and 1.00 atm.

   $$(C)$$ If current is doubled but copper efficiency falls to 60.0%, calculate the new time for the same copper mass and compare it with the original time.

   $$(D)$$ An ideal reversible cell driving the desired reaction would require 0.300 V under its operating conditions. If the external source actually supplies 1.20 V throughout the original run, compare desired-reaction electrical work with total supplied energy.

:::solution
$$(A)$$ Copper moles are $$3.00/63.55=0.04721$$. The useful charge is $$q_{Cu}=2F(0.04721)=9.11\times10^3\ C$$. Because this is only 75.0% of supplied charge, $$q_{total}=q_{Cu}/0.750=1.21\times10^4\ C$$. Time is $$q_{total}/I=4.86\times10^3\ s$$, or 81.0 min. Efficiency reduces useful charge, not the two-electron requirement per copper atom.

$$(B)$$ Hydrogen receives $$0.250q_{total}=3.04\times10^3\ C$$. The half-reaction $$\mathrm{2H^++2e^-\rightarrow H_2}$$ requires two electrons per molecule, so $$n_{H_2}=q_{H_2}/(2F)=0.0157\ \mathrm{mol}$$. Its dry ideal-gas volume is $$nRT/P=0.0157(0.0821)(298)=0.385\ L$$. No water-vapor correction is needed because the requested gas is dry.

$$(C)$$ The useful charge remains fixed by the target mass. The original useful current is $$2.50(0.750)=1.875\ A$$, while the new useful current is $$5.00(0.600)=3.00\ A$$. Thus $$t_{new}/t_{old}=1.875/3.00=0.625$$ and $$t_{new}=3.04\times10^3\ s$$, about 50.6 min. Doubling total current does not halve time when efficiency also changes.

$$(D)$$ The reversible work for the copper amount is $$w_{desired}=q_{Cu}(0.300)=2.73\ \mathrm{kJ}$$. Actual source energy is $$q_{total}(1.20)=14.6\ \mathrm{kJ}$$. Their ratio is $$0.750(0.300/1.20)=0.1875$$, about 18.8%. Both competing reactions and operating above the reversible voltage reduce the fraction of supplied energy associated with the desired reversible chemical change.
